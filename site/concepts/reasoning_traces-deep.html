Reasoning traces — how letting a model write its working gets harder problems right

When you ask a person a hard question — how many rooms in a house with 3 floors, each with 4 rooms, plus an attic — they often work through it step by step on paper. They write out the intermediate steps and build toward the answer. If you force them to blurt the answer instantly without writing anything down, they make more mistakes. Language models face the same problem. When asked a multi-step math question, a small model forced to answer immediately often blurts a wrong guess. But if you let it write out its working first — what we call a chain of thought, or reasoning trace — before giving the final answer, it does better. Here is the idea, a real run showing it rescued some problems but not all, why writing helps, where it breaks down, and why this single pattern became the foundation for reasoning models like OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1.

1 · The problem — small models blurt and guess wrong

Answering a hard question takes more than one step. For 'Alice has 3 apples. Bob has twice as many. How many do they have together?', the model must find Bob's count (6), then add (3 + 6 = 9). If the model is small and made to give a number immediately, it often skips the work and takes a guess — maybe it blurts 8 or 15. It has no room to reason; every token it produces eats into its budget, and a small model is already pushing its limits.

So the need is clear: how can a model on a hard problem get a chance to show its working before it commits to the final answer?

2 · The reframe — let it write a chain of thought first, then the answer

The move is simple and astonishing. Instead of asking 'What is the answer?' ask the model two things in sequence. First: 'Think through this step by step — what is happening at each point?' Let the model write out a chain of thought, as many tokens as it needs. Only after it has finished can you ask 'Given what you just wrote, what is the final answer?' The model now has the first attempt — the chain of thought — sitting there on the page. When it writes the final line, it can lean on what it already worked out. No retraining needed. Same model. The only change is the prompt: give it room to think first. This technique is called reasoning traces (or chain of thought when it is written all at once, or step-by-step thinking when you make it explicit). It reframes the problem from 'guess the answer now' to 'show your work, then answer'.

3 · Seen on a real run — 4 more problems solved, but 12 ramble to the end

We ran the real Qwen3-0.6B model — a small model with 600 million learned numbers — on 30 grade-school math problems from GSM8K (a benchmark of problems like 'Jenny has 5 crayons. Her friend gives her 2 more. How many does Jenny have now?'). We graded each answer as right or wrong. We measured it two ways: first, asking it to answer directly; second, asking it to write a chain of thought first, then the answer.

The model went from 14 right to 18 right — it rescued 4 problems. None broke (it did not lose any it had gotten right). But there is a cost.

4 · Why writing the work helps

The core reason is computation in paper. When the model writes a chain of thought, each written token becomes something the model can look back at. Instead of the hard leap — 'I must figure out this entire multi-step problem in one jump' — the model breaks it into easier pieces: 'I have the first step written down; now figure out the second.' Each step is a smaller problem. More writing means more scaffolding — more places to lean.

A second reason is honesty. If a model is going to guess wrong anyway, having it write its reasoning first makes the wrong answer visible. When it writes 'Bob has 6' and then 'so together we have 8', anyone reading can spot where it went wrong (it should have said 9). This transparency does not directly fix the wrong answer, but it makes errors trackable — and humans can correct them or route problems that need accuracy to a tool.

A third reason: a chain of thought often contains partial credit. Even if the final boxed answer is wrong, the model might have gotten three of four steps right. A careful grader can see this; the single final number alone never shows it.

5 · The one real weakness — it rambles on hard problems and still misses them

The model is not magic. On the 12 hardest problems, even when given room to think, it rambled all the way to 2048 tokens and did not settle on an answer. That is a failure mode: it had the room to think and used it, but still did not solve the problem. It filled the space with more thinking without getting closer to the truth.

And even with the chain of thought, the model still got 12 out of 30 wrong. That is 40% failure on grade-school math. A human would do far better. The problem is that a small model (600 million numbers) is still a small model; reasoning traces help it use its capacity better, but they do not make it smarter than it is. On the truly hard ones, the model is simply not powerful enough — more writing does not unlock knowledge it does not have.

6 · The surprise — this one pattern became the foundation of reasoning models

Reasoning traces sound like a tiny change — just ask the model to think first. But the field noticed: it works, it is cheap (no retraining), and it is reproducible. OpenAI took this pattern and wrapped it into reinforcement learning, training models to write longer, better reasoning. DeepSeek-R1 pushed it further with process reward (grading each reasoning step, not just the final answer). These models — the new 'reasoning models' that can 'think' for seconds or minutes before answering — all rest on this foundation: external computation in tokens. Writing out the work externalizes what the model cannot do in its head.

The same pattern appears in fine-tuning (when a teacher writes out worked solutions, a student learns faster) and in agents and tools (when a model can call out to exact calculations, it bypasses its own arithmetic mistakes). The common thread: give the model — or the system using the model — room to break hard problems into steps and write each step down.
