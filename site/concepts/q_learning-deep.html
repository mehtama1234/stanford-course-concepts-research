Q-Learning — turning random flailing into optimal moves by learning action values

An agent dropped into an unknown world gets only a trickle of reward and no instructions. How does it figure out which moves lead to good outcomes far in the future? The answer lies in Q-learning: keep a running score for each move in each situation — its long-term value — and update it whenever reality surprises you. The value of a move should equal the immediate reward plus the value of where it lands. Do this over many episodes and accurate values ripple back from the goal through the states between. Here is the idea, a real experiment on a gridworld showing an agent go from taking 56 steps to stumble to the goal down to the optimal 10, why bootstrapping from your own estimates works, the hard limits when the world grows large, and where this core idea lives in modern deep reinforcement learning.

1 · The problem — how do you learn from sparse, delayed rewards?

Imagine a robot in a maze. It gets a small penalty for each step it takes and a big reward when it finds the exit. After wandering for minutes and taking hundreds of steps, it finally stumbles into the goal. Now what? The exit was 200 steps ago, but the reward just arrived. How does the robot figure out which of those 200 moves were actually good? Random luck plays a huge role — a move might have led nowhere, but the robot took another move afterward that corrected course. Simply giving credit to every move that came before the reward would teach it to repeat bad moves too.

The need is urgent: when rewards arrive late and rarely, how can an agent figure out which moves actually lead to good outcomes far in the future?

2 · The reframe — bootstrap from your own estimates

Here is the key move. Keep a running estimate of the value of each move in each situation. For instance, imagine you are standing in square (3,5) of a maze and considering moving left. Before you try it, your estimate might be 'if I move left here, my long-term reward will be -5 on average' (negative because of the step penalties). Now you make the move and land in square (3,4). You immediately collect a tiny reward for one step — say, -1 — and now you ask: what does my estimate say the value is from (3,4) forward? Say the estimate is -7. Then the value of moving left from (3,5) should be: one step's reward (-1) plus the value ahead (-7), which is -8. Your old estimate was -5, but reality just told you it should be closer to -8. Adjust your estimate down. Repeat this across thousands of episodes, and correct estimates flow backward: the squares right before the goal get high estimates, then the squares before those inherit those high values, and so on. Each state's value slowly absorbs the value of the goal through the states between. This is called bootstrapping — using your own estimates to improve your estimates. The algorithm is Q-learning.

3 · Seen on a real run — from random stumbling to optimal path

We built a 6-by-6 gridworld where each step costs -1, the goal in one corner awards +10, and the optimal path is 10 steps. An untrained agent acts randomly. We then trained it with Q-learning: the agent explores by trial and error, and each time it lands somewhere, it updates its value estimates using the bootstrapping rule — immediate reward plus the estimated value of the next state. After training, we ask it to follow the highest-value move in each state (the greedy policy). Here is what we measured.

The discovered fact: the untrained agent averages 56 steps to stumble to the goal; after training, it takes 10 steps — the optimal path — by following the highest-value move each time. No hand-written rules, no map given in advance. Pure trial-and-error learning reshaped chaotic exploration into reliable optimal behavior.

4 · Why bootstrapping works — value ripples backward through the world

The core insight is this: the value of being in a state is only useful if you know what comes next. Early on, the agent's estimates for most states are wrong because it has not explored everywhere yet. But the goal state gets the right value quickly — it is where big rewards happen. As the agent updates more episodes, states right before the goal inherit the goal's high value, because the bootstrap rule says 'value of moving here = reward + value of next state'. Then states two moves before the goal inherit their value, and so on. Over hundreds of episodes, the accurate values ripple backward like a wave from the reward, reaching farther and farther into the past. Every episode tightens the estimates a little more. Eventually, even the starting state absorbs a high-value signal: the model knows 'if I move correctly from here, I will get a good reward many steps away.'

5 · The one real weakness — the explosion of state-action pairs

The catch is storage and time. Q-learning keeps a table: one row for each state, one column for each possible move. For the 6x6 gridworld with 4 directions, that is 36 states times 4 actions = 144 entries. Simple. But a real robot in the world has nearly infinite states — every position, angle, and sensor reading is different. A self-driving car has a state space so large you could not store it in a table if you had a trillion computers. This is why tabular Q-learning does not work for real problems. The solution is to approximate the values with a neural network instead of a table. But networks are harder to train stably — bootstrapping from estimates can go wrong if the network is large and the estimates are wrong. This is why deep reinforcement learning is much trickier than tabular Q-learning.

6 · The surprise — the idea lives on in deep Q-networks and almost all of modern RL

Q-learning with a deep network behind it is called a Deep Q-Network (DQN), and it learned to play Atari games from raw pixels in 2013 — a landmark moment for AI. Even though deep reinforcement learning now has many flavors — policy gradients that directly learn moves rather than values, actor-critic methods that mix the two, model-based methods that learn to plan — all of them trace back to this core idea: if you know the value of a state, you can learn better by comparing your old guess to a reward plus your new guess. The principle of bootstrapping, learning from your own estimates, underlies most of modern reinforcement learning.

the run recipe — reproduce every number above

Run a 6x6 gridworld with -1 reward per step and +10 at the goal (optimal path = 10 steps). Train an agent with Q-learning: after each action, update the Q-value as Q(state, action) = immediate reward + discount * max Q-value of the next state. For the first 10 episodes, the agent explores randomly and measures average steps to goal. After training on all episodes, measure steps taken by greedily picking the highest-Q-value move in each state.

Q(s, a) = R(s, a) + gamma * max_a' Q(s', a') where s' is the next state after taking action a in state s R(s, a) is the immediate reward, gamma is the discount factor Update rule: Q_new = Q_old + alpha * (R + gamma * max Q(s') - Q_old) Measured: 56 steps early (untrained) -> 10 steps after training (optimal)
