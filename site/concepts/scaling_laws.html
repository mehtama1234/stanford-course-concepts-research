Scaling Laws

When you feed an AI system more data or computation, its performance improves in predictable patterns that you can measure and plot.

What real-world problem is this about?

You're running a photo app that learns to recognize faces. Adding smarter algorithms helps—but so does feeding it more photos and letting it practice longer. Which should you invest in next? You need a way to predict whether spending more on computing power, collecting more photos, or both will actually improve results.

Why does this problem exist?

Any learning system faces finite resources: processing power, training time, and examples to learn from. When you're bottlenecked by lack of data, throwing more compute at the problem doesn't unlock new abilities—you're stuck teaching the same lessons over and over. When you're bottlenecked by weak hardware, adding data doesn't help. The system is forced to reveal which bottleneck is tightest right now, and what happens when you loosen it.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A scaling law plots a curve where the horizontal axis is some quantity you can increase (data size, computing budget, model parameters—the measurable things) and the vertical axis is performance loss (how wrong predictions are). Empirically, this curve often follows a power law: loss ∝ (1 / size^α), where α is a slope that captures how steeply performance improves. The formula tells you: if you double your resources, loss shrinks by a factor of 2^α. Why a power law? Because most learning systems don't hit walls suddenly; instead, harder examples require exponentially more data to master, and the compounding effect produces this smooth algebraic curve.

Why is this concept important?

Once you know your scaling law, you can plan: which investment returns the most gain per dollar? Doubling model size costs one kind of money; doubling data costs another. The curve tells you which lever to pull next and whether progress will stall or accelerate as you grow. It also explains emergent abilities—new skills that appear only after crossing certain scales because earlier sizes simply weren't large enough to internalize those patterns.

What breaks without it?

Without scaling laws, teams guess blindly: throw money at the problem and hope. You might overtrain a small model on data it's already memorized, waste resources on architecture tweaks that don't matter, or stop scaling too early thinking progress has plateaued when you're actually just hitting a temporary bottleneck.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might assume: if a model trained on 1 million examples reaches 80% accuracy, then one trained on 2 million will reach 80.5% or some tiny predictable step. Or: bigger models always need more data proportionally. But reality is messier—the relationship is nonlinear, and diminishing returns kick in at different points depending on which resource you change.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

The naive 'linear improvement' approach fails because it misses the compound effect of bottlenecks. If a model is starving for data, adding compute barely helps. If it's starving for parameters, adding data without model capacity wastes the data. The simple guess also ignores that some abilities (like reasoning over long chains) only emerge after crossing a threshold—improvement is not smooth everywhere.

The Mathematical Object

The scaling curve itself is the object you inspect and modify. It is a table or fitted equation that maps (data size, model size, compute budget) to expected loss. You can read off it: 'if I invest $X more, what loss should I expect?' It's concrete because you can plot it on paper or log into a database and look up the trade-off in real time.

The Operation

First, run training experiments at several different scales—say, 100M, 1B, 10B parameters—keeping everything else fixed. Measure final loss at each scale. Plot those points on a graph (horizontal axis = scale, vertical axis = loss). Fit a power-law curve through the points. This curve is now your predictor: input a new scale, read off the expected loss. Before committing resources, simulate the investment: 'if we triple compute, the curve predicts loss shrinks by a factor of 2^α. Is that worth the cost?'

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose a language model trained on 10 billion words reaches a loss of 4.0. You train again on 100 billion words and get loss 2.8. Plot those two points: (10B, 4.0) and (100B, 2.8). A power law fit suggests loss ∝ 1/(data^0.25). Using this curve, if you had trained on 1 trillion words, you'd predict loss ≈ 2.0. Before spending months training at that scale, you check the curve: is halving loss worth the 10× compute budget? If yes, commit. If no, invest in data quality or model architecture instead. The lecture emphasizes that mixture-of-experts models choose this trade-off: activate only a fraction of parameters per example, trading a tiny loss increase for vastly cheaper inference, because the scaling curve told them inference cost mattered as much as training loss.

What the Lectures Emphasize

Across Stanford lectures, scale appears as an engineering constraint, not just a number on a leaderboard. CME295 discusses mixture-of-experts precisely because scaling laws revealed that all-parameter activation becomes prohibitively expensive at inference; experts let you activate only the relevant subset and keep using larger models. CS224R frames modern RL-for-LLMs progress as riding a wave of larger models and far more training compute—a consistent trend where the community plots scaling curves and invests aggressively because those curves promise payoff. CME296 connects parameter count and training compute to image quality in diffusion models. The shared lesson: measure scaling, predict, then allocate resources where the curve says gains are steepest.

Common Misunderstanding

People often think 'scaling laws prove that bigger is always better,' or 'scaling is universal.' In reality, scaling is conditional. A scaling law holds for a specific architecture, dataset distribution, and evaluation metric—change any of those, the curve shifts. Data quality, training stability, inference constraints, and what you're actually trying to measure all redirect where growth goes. A curve predicts efficiently only within its domain; extrapolate too far, and diminishing returns or phase transitions catch you off guard.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In new papers, spot scaling-law reasoning whenever authors: plot loss or accuracy against data size, model parameters, or compute budget; discuss why they chose a particular model size for their compute budget; mention 'compute-optimal' choices; describe mixture-of-experts, sparse activation, or other schemes designed to scale without paying the full inference cost; or claim that a new ability (like chain-of-thought reasoning) emerges only at scale. The underlying pattern is always: measure, fit a curve, predict, and let the curve guide resource allocation.

Connected Concepts

Pretraining across CME295 and CS224R rests on scaling laws—knowing how large a model and dataset you need for the capabilities you want downstream. Evaluation in CME296 depends on scaling laws because you must know whether improved metrics with more training compute reflect real progress or are just artifacts of overfitting or metric saturation. Emergence (sudden appearance of new skills) connects to scaling because emergence is just the point on the curve where a bottleneck shifts and the model suddenly has enough capacity or data to represent something previously locked away. In all three courses, the pattern is: grow what matters, measure the trade-offs, and let the curve guide you.
