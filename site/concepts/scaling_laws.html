Scaling Laws

When you give an AI system more examples to learn from, more computing power, or larger internal structures—its ability to make correct predictions improves in a way you can measure and predict ahead of time.

What real-world problem is this about?

You're building a system that recognizes faces in photos. You can improve it by writing smarter code, but also by showing it more photos and letting it practice longer. Your budget is limited. Should you spend money on faster computers, buy more photos, hire people to label more data, or split the budget? You need a concrete way to predict: which choice gives the most improvement per dollar spent?

Why does this problem exist?

Any learning system has real limits: computing power costs money, time costs money, and collecting examples costs money. Imagine you're teaching a person to recognize dog breeds. If you have zero photos and unlimited time, adding more time doesn't help. If you have millions of photos but a slow brain, adding more photos doesn't help either. A learning system works the same way—it always hits one bottleneck first. When you give it more of whatever it's bottlenecked on, abilities jump forward. When you give it more of what isn't bottlenecked, nothing changes. The system reveals which limit is tightest right now, and what happens when you loosen it.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

You collect data from experiments: train on 100 million examples, measure how often predictions are wrong; train on 1 billion examples, measure again; train on 10 billion, measure again. Plot these three points on a graph—horizontal axis is how many examples, vertical axis is how often the system is wrong. The points form a curve. That curve usually follows a simple rule called a power law: if you multiply the examples by 10, the error shrinks by a fixed percentage (say 20% or 30%, not 1%). The curve lets you predict: if I buy 100 times more examples, my error should shrink by roughly that fixed percentage three times in a row. Why does this pattern show up? Because when a system learns something new, it needs roughly twice as many examples as the last harder thing it learned—that compound effect naturally creates this smooth curve.

Why is this concept important?

Once you know the curve, you can make real money decisions: spending $1 million on faster computers or on collecting better examples? The curve tells you which one gives more improvement. It also predicts whether a team will reach their goal: if the curve shows 'error drops 10% per 10× more examples,' and you're stuck at 15% error with billions of examples, then maybe speed isn't the answer—maybe the system needs a completely different design. The curve also explains why some systems suddenly learn to do things they couldn't do before—they had to reach a certain size first.

What breaks without it?

Teams build without scaling laws by throwing money at problems blindly: 'let's just buy more compute.' They might train a small system on examples it's already memorized (wasting time), spend months tweaking the code for 1% gains when the curve says doubling the data gives 10%, or give up on a project when they're actually just hitting a temporary bottleneck and should keep growing. Bad spending decisions cost millions.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might think: a model trained on 1 million photos reaches 80% accuracy, so one trained on 2 million will reach 80.5%, then 81% at 3 million—a steady march. Or: if a model has twice as many internal dials, it automatically needs twice as much data. But real data follows no such rule—the relationship is crooked and invisible until you plot it. Sometimes doubling the data barely helps; sometimes it transforms the system. The connection isn't obvious.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Guessing 'linear improvement' fails because bottlenecks compound. If a model is starving for data (too few examples), adding computing power is useless—the bottleneck stays. If it's starving for internal dials to tune, adding more examples is wasted because it can't turn those examples into learned patterns. The guess also misses threshold effects: some things (like reasoning through a long chain of logic) only work after a model reaches a certain size. Below that size, improvement is flat; cross the threshold, improvement jumps. Linear thinking misses both the bottleneck collisions and the jumps.

The Mathematical Object

The scaling curve is a concrete table or equation. One column lists sizes (100M parameters, 1B parameters, 10B parameters). Another column lists the error you actually measured at that size. Draw those points on a piece of paper; the curve through them is the object. You can look at it and read off: 'if I go to 100 billion parameters, I should expect roughly this error.' It's not abstract—you write it down, plot it, or store it in a spreadsheet. When a team wants to decide what to build next, they pull up the curve and see: what does it predict if we spend more?

The Operation

Run training experiments at several sizes—for instance, 100 million internal dials, then 1 billion, then 10 billion—while keeping everything else the same (same data, same learning method, same architecture). Record the error at each size. Plot these pairs on graph paper: horizontal is size, vertical is error. You'll see a curve shape. Fit a power law through it—software or a calculator can do this in seconds. This curve is now your prediction tool. Want to know what happens at 50 billion parameters? Input 50 billion into the equation; it outputs the expected error. Before a team commits months of expensive compute, they check the curve: 'if we spend $X more, error shrinks by this much—is that worth it?'

Worked Mini-Example

A team trains language models at five sizes: 100M parameters (error=4.2), 1B (error=2.8), 10B (error=1.8), 100B (error=1.1), 1T (error=0.67). Plotting these on log-log axes and fitting a power law: error ≈ 2.8 / (params_in_billions ^ 0.2). Using this curve, they predict: 10T parameters → error ≈ 0.44. A 10× increase in size (100B to 1T) cut error by about 1.6× (1.1 to 0.67). Will another 10× (1T to 10T) help as much? The power law says error drops only about a third more (0.67 to ~0.44). This improvement costs them $60 million in compute and 6 months. They ask: is 33% error reduction worth that? Previous decision: yes. But at the 10T scale, they discover an architectural bottleneck: distributed training efficiency drops from 95% to 60% (GPU-to-GPU communication overhead). So tripling compute yields only 1.8× actual speedup, not 3×. The scaling law predicted correctly, but didn't capture the infrastructure plateau. They pivot: keep the model at 1T but redesign it as a mixture-of-experts (only 20% of weights activate per example). Error hits 0.72 (slightly worse), but inference cost drops 80%, generating 5× more samples per dollar. The scaling analysis guided the architectural choice.

What the Lectures Emphasize

Across the Stanford courses, scaling is framed as an engineering decision, not a prestige metric. One course (CME295) covers mixture-of-experts specifically because scaling laws revealed a hard fact: activating every internal dial becomes prohibitively expensive when models grow huge; so teams activate only the relevant subset per example, letting you use larger models while keeping prediction cost reasonable. Another course (CS224R) treats modern AI progress as riding a wave: bigger models plus way more training compute equals better behavior—a consistent trend where the community plots these curves and bets billions because the curves promise payoff. A third course (CME296) connects parameter count and training compute directly to image quality. The shared message across all three: measure how performance changes with scale, predict the next step, then invest your money where the curve says gains are steepest.

Common Misunderstanding

People often assume 'scaling laws prove bigger is always better' or 'scaling works everywhere the same way.' In reality, a scaling law is conditional on the architecture you pick, the distribution of data you have, and what you measure. Change any of those, the curve shifts. Data quality (garbage in, garbage out), training stability (some techniques work better at scale), inference speed constraints (you might not afford prediction costs), and what you actually try to measure—all redirect the curve's shape. A curve predicts reliably only within its original setting. Push it far beyond that setting, and diminishing returns or sudden phase transitions blindside you.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When reading new research papers, spot scaling-law thinking if the authors: plot error or accuracy against data volume, parameter count, or compute budget; explain why they chose a specific model size based on their compute budget; use the phrase 'compute-optimal' or discuss similar trade-offs; describe mixture-of-experts, sparse activation, or other designs built explicitly to scale inference cost down; or claim a new ability (like reasoning through long chains, or generating coherent stories) emerges only after reaching a certain scale. These are all symptoms of the same underlying pattern: measure, fit a curve, predict, and let the curve guide resource allocation.

Connected Concepts

Pretraining strategy (CME295 and CS224R) assumes you know the scaling curve—how large a model and dataset you need to unlock the downstream abilities you want. Evaluation (CME296) uses scaling laws because you must distinguish real progress from artifacts: did accuracy improve because we genuinely trained better, or just because we trained for longer and happen to be measuring at a different point on the saturation curve? Emergence (the sudden appearance of new capabilities) happens because emergence is just the moment on the scaling curve where a bottleneck shifts and the model finally has enough internal capacity or enough examples to represent a pattern it was locked out of before. In all three courses, the same pattern repeats: identify what matters to measure, grow the resources that matter, map the trade-offs, and use the curve to guide decisions.
