Scaling Laws

Regular patterns in what improves when models, data, and compute grow.

What real-world problem is this about?

The problem is that building large models is expensive, so teams need a way to predict whether more scale is worth it.

Why does this problem exist?

Capacity, data, and compute are bottlenecks. When one bottleneck is relaxed, another can become the limiter.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A scaling law fits a curve between resources and loss or capability. The curve is a planning tool, not a law of nature.

Why is this concept important?

It guides training budgets and helps explain why some abilities appear only after enough practice and capacity.

What breaks without it?

Scaling decisions become guesswork, and systems may be undertrained or wastefully oversized.

Naive Starting Point

Start with the simplest version: The problem is that building large models is expensive, so teams need a way to predict whether more scale is worth it. A beginner might try to solve this with a fixed rule, a lookup table, or one direct guess. That works only while the situation is tiny and clean.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

The simple approach breaks because capacity, data, and compute are bottlenecks. when one bottleneck is relaxed, another can become the limiter. Once the input grows, feedback arrives late, or many explanations compete, the system needs a reusable mechanism instead of a special case.

The Mathematical Object

The useful object is the thing the model can change or compare while learning Scaling Laws: A scaling law fits a curve between resources and loss or capability. The curve is a planning tool, not a law of nature. Think of it as the handle the algorithm can grab.

The Operation

The operation is to apply that object repeatedly: read the current situation, produce a numerical signal, and use feedback or comparison to adjust the next step. For Scaling Laws, this is the move that turns a vague goal into an executable procedure.

Worked Mini-Example

Mini-example: a team can double model parameters, double data, or spend more inference compute, but each choice costs money. Scaling laws ask whether larger resources predictably reduce loss or unlock behavior. CME295 discusses mixture-of-experts as a way to scale LLMs without activating every parameter at inference time. CS224R notes the broader trend of putting more compute into larger models when motivating RL for LLMs.

What the Lectures Emphasize

Across the corpus, scale appears as both opportunity and constraint. CME295 frames architecture choices such as sparse experts around the cost of using all parameters. CS224R frames large-model progress around more compute. CME296 architecture and training lectures connect parameter count, training choices, and generative quality. The shared emphasis is that scale changes engineering trade-offs, not just leaderboard numbers.

Common Misunderstanding

A common misunderstanding is that scaling laws say bigger is always better. Scaling is conditional: data quality, compute budget, architecture, inference cost, and evaluation target all matter. A curve can guide planning without proving that every capability will improve smoothly.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In a new paper, recognize scaling-law reasoning whenever authors relate loss, capability, data size, parameter count, compute, active parameters, or inference-time work. The shared primitive is bottleneck shifting: once one limit is relaxed, another limit may become the thing controlling progress.

Connected Concepts

The same primitive reappears near pretraining, emergence, evaluation. Across the three courses, the shared pattern is scale, feedback: choose what to keep, how to move, what feedback means, or how to measure whether the behavior really improved.
