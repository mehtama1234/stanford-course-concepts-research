Actor-Critic — how a critic baseline makes policy learning nearly noise-free

A policy gradient agent learns by trial and error: take an action, see how well it worked, nudge the policy in that direction. The problem is noise. One big reward or one unlucky episode swings the learning signal wildly and can stall the agent. Actor-Critic solves this by running two learners in tandem: an 'actor' (the policy that picks moves) and a 'critic' (a running estimate of the reward you'd expect from each situation). Instead of learning from the raw outcome, the actor learns only from the surprise — how much better or worse than expected — which is far quieter. Here is the idea, measurements on a real gridworld run showing the sharp jump in performance, why subtracting an expectation cleans the signal, where this setup strains, and how it became the foundation of modern deep reinforcement learning.

1 · The problem — raw rewards are noisy, one big event swings everything

Policy gradient learning works: nudge the policy toward moves that led to good outcomes and away from moves that led to bad ones. But the signal is brutal. Suppose on one trial the agent gets a reward of 50 and on another it gets 10. The learning step sized by the raw difference is huge and unstable — a single lucky episode or single disaster can swing the update wildly and undo progress. The agent can stall for many episodes because normal randomness in rewards drowns the true direction of improvement. The need is real: how can you learn from outcomes without being thrown off by every noise spike?

2 · The reframe — learn from the surprise, not the raw score

The key insight is to separate the size of the reward from whether it was surprising. Instead of learning from the raw outcome, learn only from the gap: how much better or worse than expected? Here is the move. Run a second learner called the critic — a function that watches the state and predicts: 'in this situation, what reward would I expect to get?' This is a running estimate, called the value, that the critic itself learns from experience. Now, when the agent gets an actual outcome, compute the temporal-difference error (the fancy term for surprise): reward actually received minus the value the critic predicted. This error, call it delta, becomes the learning signal. If the agent got what it expected, delta is near zero and the policy barely budges — no signal in a typical outcome. If the agent did much better than expected, delta is big and positive and the move gets a strong push. If it did much worse, the move gets pushed away. The actor is the policy using these delta signals; the critic is the value function that computes the baseline. The breakthrough: delta is far quieter than raw rewards because it removes the constant 'everything rewards around N' noise, leaving only the true signal of which moves beat expectations.

3 · Seen on a real run — critic baseline pushes from good to optimal

We ran the same policy-gradient learning algorithm twice on a 4 by 4 gridworld (robot needs to reach a goal in roughly 4 steps; each step costs -1, the goal gives +5): once without a critic, once with a critic baseline. Both ran the same policy gradient method for the same number of episodes and we took the median of 7 runs to smooth out randomness. The measure: what fraction of probability mass ended up on the two goal-ward moves, and what was the final average return per episode?

The discovered fact: plain policy gradient reaches goal-ward probability 0.86 and final return -8; adding a critic reaches goal-ward probability 1.00 and final return +5, which is optimal. The critic subtracts the expected value so the learning signal becomes pure surprise. Where raw policy gradient found a decent direction but stalled, actor-critic descended all the way to the optimal path.

4 · Why the baseline sharpens learning

The reason is signal clarity. With raw rewards, every update is scaled by the actual outcome: get 100 and the move gets a large bump, get 10 and it gets a small one, even if both are equally good relative to what you expected. Adding a baseline subtracts the 'always getting something around N' part of every reward, leaving only the part that varies: 'this time I beat expectations, this time I missed.' The critic is a learnable baseline — it finds the expected value and the actor uses only the deviation from that prediction as the learning signal. This is called the temporal-difference or TD advantage: the surprise. Surprise signals are sparse and clean. A typical, expected outcome gives a surprise near zero, so the policy barely moves — no wasted updates. An outcome that beats expectations gives positive surprise and a clear push. An outcome worse than expected gives negative surprise and a clear push away. The noise from 'how much does this outcome reward in absolute terms' is gone. Only the pattern of 'was this better or worse than typical' survives. That is why, in the run, the critic version steers directly to optimal while the plain version wanders.

5 · The one real weakness — now you must train two things, and a bad critic hurts

The cost is complexity. With plain policy gradient, you tune one learner and one learning rate. With actor-critic, you now train both a policy and a value function, which means two separate learning rates, two separate architectures, and two sources of instability. A critic that is poorly trained gives a misleading baseline — if the critic over-estimates value, the agent thinks it is doing worse than it is and stops improving; if it under-estimates, the learning signal gets inflated and the agent thrashes. Balancing the speed at which the actor and critic learn is finicky: if the critic learns much faster than the actor, the baseline chases the actual rewards and the surprise signal vanishes; if the actor learns much faster, the critic falls behind and its predictions are always stale. In simple toy problems like the gridworld, these trade-offs are barely visible. In high-dimensional problems with sparse rewards, tuning both learners is a major burden and a common source of training failures.

6 · The surprise — actor-critic methods are the workhorses of modern deep RL

This simple idea — an actor guided by a critic's baseline estimate — became the engine of practical deep reinforcement learning. Variants appear everywhere: A2C (synchronous version of Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic, run on many parallel actors at once); PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization, adds a safety constraint so the policy does not shift too far); SAC (Soft Actor-Critic, adds entropy to encourage exploration). These methods power robot learning, game-playing agents, and the reward models that grade language model outputs in RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) — the training method behind modern aligned large language models. The one-sentence lesson: when you want a policy to learn from experience, do not feed it raw rewards — give it the surprise relative to expectations. Learn the expectations separately and use the gap as the signal. That single move is why modern deep RL works at all.
