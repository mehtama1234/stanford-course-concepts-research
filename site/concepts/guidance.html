Guidance

A way to point a program that makes pictures or text in the direction you want. The program naturally creates plausible things—a bear might look realistic but forget the book you asked for. Guidance adds a nudge at each step of creation that says: "include the book," so the final result both looks real and matches your request.

What real-world problem is this about?

You ask a friend to draw a teddy bear reading a book. They return with a beautiful, realistic bear—but no book. The drawing is skillful, and everything about the bear is correct, but it completely missed the second half of what you asked for. You need the bear to stay realistic and skillful while actually including the book.

Why does this problem exist?

A program that generates images learns by studying millions of real pictures. It becomes very good at making a realistic bear, a realistic forest, or a realistic person. But 'realistic' and 'what you asked for' are not the same thing. When you add a request—like 'bear reading a book' or 'sunset in winter'—the program faces a conflict: keep everything looking natural and real (based on what it learned), while also satisfy your specific request. There is no single fixed rule that works for every request. You need a way to gently push the creation process toward your request at every single moment while it is building the image.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Image creation happens step by step: the program starts with noise and gradually shapes it into a real image by predicting which way to remove the noise. Guidance changes this prediction by adding information from a score—a number that measures how well the current partial image matches your request. Imagine the program wants to move in direction A (based on what it learned), but your request says move in direction B. Guidance computes direction B by asking: "If I changed this partial image a little, would it match your request better?" Then it blends both directions together using a weight. The final move is: original direction A, plus (weight times how much to move toward direction B). If the weight is too small, the program ignores your request. If it is too large, the image looks distorted and unnatural because the program is being forced too hard against what it learned.

Why is this concept important?

Without guidance, a program that makes pictures from text would generate things that look real and pretty but have nothing to do with what you asked for. Guidance is what makes these programs useful—what lets you ask for a teddy bear reading a book and actually get both the bear and the book. It transforms the technology from a research experiment into a tool you can actually use.

What breaks without it?

The program produces an image that is coherent and realistic but completely misses your request. A beautiful bear sitting alone. A sunset in orange when you asked for winter blue. The image does not break any rule of how the real world works, but it fails your actual goal because it does not address what you asked for.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might think: why not train a separate program that takes text directly and outputs images? Or collect examples of 'teddy bear with book' and look them up in a table when someone asks? These work on tiny homework problems with limited, clean requests. But real requests are endless and surprising—you cannot train a new program for every possible text, and you cannot store and look up a picture for every combination of words someone might ask for.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Direct mapping (one text-to-image program) requires retraining from scratch for every new type of request, which is expensive and slow. A lookup table requires pre-collecting millions of pictures for every combination of words—impossible. A simple rule like 'just match the label' forces the program so hard against what it learned that the image becomes distorted or visually broken. Users ask for endless variations, unusual combinations, and specific details that do not exist in pre-made examples. The system always runs out of coverage.

The Mathematical Object

A score function—another program, or a mathematical rule, that takes a partial image (the image halfway through being created) and outputs a single number measuring how well it matches your request. For text requests, this might score how similar the image is to the words you typed. For safety, it might detect whether the image contains something harmful. This score function must be smooth: you can ask 'if I change the image slightly, does the score go up or down?' and measure exactly how much and in which direction. This measurement is the gradient—the slope showing which way to push the image to improve.

The Operation

Here is what happens at each step of image creation: (1) the program picks a direction based on what it learned about making realistic images; (2) you compute your score on the partial image—how well does it match your request right now?; (3) you measure the gradient—the direction that would make the score higher; (4) you blend the two directions using a strength control knob, producing a combined move that respects both realism and your request; (5) take one small step in that combined direction; (6) repeat until the image is done. The strength knob is your guidance weight: turn it high to follow your request closely, turn it low to trust what the program learned.

Worked Mini-Example

You request 'a green apple.' A diffusion model starts with 256×256 random noise and refines it in 50 steps. At step 25, the model's inherent knowledge generates an apple-like shape and coloring—but it is red (which is common in training data). Your guidance scorer runs on the step-25 image and computes: 'greenness score' = 0.2/1.0 (20% of pixels are actually green). It calculates the gradient (direction to make pixels greener), finding that 'shift pixel colors toward (0, 255, 0)' increases the score. The guidance weight λ is set to 7.5. The model's natural next move (step 26) would be 'keep this apple-realistic.' The guidance direction is 'shift colors greener.' You blend them: 0.925 × (model direction) + 7.5 × (guidance direction) = combined direction. Step 26 moves the apple toward both realism and green. By step 50, the apple is both recognizable as an apple and visibly green (87% greenness). If you set λ = 0 (no guidance), the apple stays red (model defaults to common training cases). If you set λ = 15 (double guidance), the apple becomes extremely green but might develop odd shapes (losing realism).

What the Lectures Emphasize

The CME296 course highlights a critical insight: a program can make beautiful, realistic pictures while completely ignoring what you asked for. Beauty and faithfulness to your request are separate problems. Classifier-free guidance is the practical method that steers the program toward your request without needing a separate judge program. The lesson is that controllable generation requires active steering during creation—not just building better, more powerful programs.

Common Misunderstanding

Many people believe a more advanced program automatically follows requests better. But a program trained only on millions of diverse images becomes skilled at making plausible things, not at respecting your words. It learned patterns like 'bears appear near trees' and 'apples are round,' but there is no direct wire from your words to the image. Guidance is the missing connection: it takes your request and feeds it directly into the creation process step by step.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Watch for methods that steer generation by measuring how well a partial output meets a goal. Classifier-free guidance compares images made with and without your prompt. Reward-guided generation uses a reward score. Safety guidance detects harmful content. Layout guidance uses spatial rules. All follow the same structure: measure how well the current draft meets your goal, compute the direction of improvement, and mix it into the generation step.

Connected Concepts

In Deep RL courses, guidance resembles reward shaping—changing the reward signal to push an agent toward desired behavior. In language model courses, prompt engineering steers the next-word prediction toward your goal by choosing input text carefully. Across all three courses, the shared idea is the same: identify a direction the program wants to go, measure progress toward your goal, and adjust the path accordingly.
