Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

Building an imaginary copy of how the world works, so you can predict what happens before you act.

What real-world problem is this about?

Imagine teaching a friend to bake bread for the first time. Trial-and-error takes all day: they mix, bake, the crust burns, they adjust, try again, wait for it to cool, adjust again. By evening they've wasted flour and spent five hours. But if they understood the rules first—'if oven is hotter, crust browns in half the time; if dough sits longer, it rises more'—they could predict the result before spending an hour baking. They'd waste less flour and get it right faster.

Why does this problem exist?

The world doesn't tell you what happens after you act—it just happens. If you could predict the outcome before you commit, you'd make better choices. A learned model gives you that prediction: 'if I do this, the world will respond that way.' Then you can test out actions in your head instead of only learning by failing in reality.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A dynamics model is a program you train to complete this sentence: 'Given where I am now (state) and what I choose to do (action), the world will look like this next' (next state). The formula s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t) + noise is just that in symbols: the state after the next step is some function f applied to your current state and action, plus randomness (because the world is never perfectly predictable). Once you have this model, you can chain predictions: imagine picking action 1, seeing the predicted state, picking action 2, seeing the next predicted state, and so on down a whole sequence. Then you pick the sequence your model predicts will be best.

Why is this concept important?

Real experiments are slow or expensive or risky: programming a robot to learn costs thousands of dollars in hardware, medical treatments take weeks to see if they worked, crashing into a wall damages the robot. With a learned model, you simulate thousands of action sequences for free inside the computer, using your model as a pretend world, before you ever touch the real thing. This lets you make smart decisions without the cost.

What breaks without it?

Without a model, you only learn from real tries. Every failure costs time or money or risk. You have no way to think ahead or explore what-if scenarios. Each new problem feels brand new because you have no mental model to transfer ideas across similar situations.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might reason: 'I'll just memorize the best action for every possible situation.' This crashes instantly when the situation shifts even slightly, or when there are billions of possible situations (like all the spots a chess piece could be).

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Pure lookup-table memorization fails because the world is too big. A robot hand can be in millions of poses; a video game board has billions of possible scenes. You cannot visit them all or write down an answer for each. Instead, you need a learned rule that works for unseen situations—a model that captures the real pattern of how actions change the world.

The Mathematical Object

The actual thing you build and keep is the dynamics model itself: a program (usually a neural network—a program with millions of tunable dials) that takes in a current scene and an action, and outputs the predicted next scene. You can save it, run it on new scenarios, and test it against real data to see if it predicts correctly.

The Operation

Five steps: First, gather real experience (try actions, see what happens). Second, train your model on that data: teach it to predict the next state given a current state and action. Third, run imaginary experiments: feed your model a sequence of actions and watch the predicted outcomes unfold. Fourth, pick the action sequence your model predicts will be best. Fifth, execute that sequence in the real world and collect new data, then loop back to improve the model further.

Worked Mini-Example

Learning to parallel-park without model-based thinking means: try to park, fail and hit the curb, back up and try again, fail again, eventually after ten attempts you succeed. With a model: after just two or three real parking tries, you build an internal rule: 'If I turn the wheel this far at this speed, the car swings this much sideways.' Now you imagine twenty different steering patterns in your mind (or run them on a computer) without moving the car once. You pick the pattern your model predicts will work. When you try it for real, it works much better because you reasoned ahead instead of bumping into the curb.

What the Lectures Emphasize

This course teaches two concrete problems: How do you train a model from real experience so it learns the true rules of the world? And once you have that model, how do you use it to plan good actions? The examples make it concrete: a robot arm learning to grasp things can't afford to fail a thousand times on the real hardware—it's too expensive. But it can try a thousand imagined grasps using a learned model, pick the best one, and only then try for real.

Common Misunderstanding

People often think: 'My model has to be perfect or it's useless.' Wrong. A slightly wrong model is usually better than no model at all. What matters is that the model gets the direction of change right. A model that says 'turn the wheel left and the car goes left' works even if it's off by 10%. A model that says nothing at all is worthless.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for model-based RL in a paper when you see: an agent learning a model of what happens next (predictions of the world); those predictions feeding into planning (what action is best?); simulated practice runs (using the model to generate fake experience) to train a decision rule; or any explicit choice between how accurate to make the model versus how deep to plan ahead. The throughline: the agent thinks ahead using a learned pretend world.

Connected Concepts

Model-based RL is woven through all three courses. In Deep RL, it's the opposite extreme from model-free (learning only from real tries). In Diffusion Models, the reverse process itself is a learned model: 'given this noisy image, what comes out when I remove a tiny bit of noise?' is predicting the next, cleaner state—same core pattern of learning sequences. In Transformers, the model learns which parts of the input text matter for predicting the next word—a lightweight learned model of patterns in sequences.
