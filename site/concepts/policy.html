Policy

A rule that maps what an agent observes to what it chooses to do.

What real-world problem is this about?

You are arranging a dinner party. Guests arrive tired or energized based on the route they took, the people they talk to, and how much help you ask them to give. Each choice you make now—who sits where, what you ask someone to do—changes the mood and events that come next. You need a consistent way to decide what to do based on what you're seeing, not a different strategy for every possible table arrangement.

Why does this problem exist?

When an action causes the world to change, you can't choose blindly or use the same fixed answer every time. The situation keeps shifting. You need a mapping—a rule that looks at the current state and says what to do next. Without it, there's no way to connect observation to choice or to improve behavior systematically.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

A policy is written as π(a|s): the probability of choosing action a given the situation s. Think of it as a function—you feed in what you see (s), and it outputs the odds of each possible action (a). If you're in situation s1, the policy might say there's a 60% chance to do action 1 and 40% chance to do action 2. Why odds instead of a single answer? Because the best move often depends on hidden details you can't see—so expressing uncertainty (a probability distribution, meaning the odds of each choice) lets the agent hedge and explore wisely. The mathematical form π(a|s) is just a conditional probability: given s is true, what is the likelihood of a?

Why is this concept important?

A policy is the central object you actually learn in reinforcement learning. Everything else—judging whether a state is good, measuring reward, running an update—builds to change the policy. Once you have it, you can run the agent forward, collect data on what happens, and improve it step by step.

What breaks without it?

If you don't name the policy explicitly, you have no way to compare two strategies or to write down what you want the agent to improve. You can't separate 'what the agent observes' from 'what it does about it,' so feedback has nowhere to attach.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might think: 'Just memorize the best action for each situation. Look up the situation in a table, pick the action, done.' This works if there are only five situations in the world and you've seen them all before.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

A lookup table fails the moment there are new situations the table never saw. If the agent encounters a state even slightly different from the memorized ones, it has no answer. There's also no way to generalize or improve—a table is either right or wrong, with no intermediate learning.

The Mathematical Object

The policy π(a|s) is a function you can actually inspect and modify. In practice, it often lives inside a neural network: you feed in a representation of state s, and the network outputs a vector of action probabilities. This function is what the algorithm tweaks during learning. You can ask: 'For this situation, what probability does the policy assign to each action?' and watch those probabilities change as the agent learns.

The Operation

To run a policy: (1) observe the current situation s; (2) query the policy π(a|s) to get the probability of each action; (3) sample an action a according to those probabilities (or pick the highest-probability action); (4) execute a and see what happens next. The output of step 3 is an action that changes the world, which determines what you observe next.

Worked Mini-Example

A robot arm sees a block on a table (situation s). The policy π(a|s) gives probabilities for four actions: push 30%, lift 50%, wait 15%, move camera 5%. The robot samples from these odds and chooses 'lift.' It lifts the block, which tips it over—now the situation has changed. On the next step, the robot sees the block on the floor (new situation s'), the policy outputs different action probabilities, and the loop repeats. After many trials and updates, the policy learns that lifting the block requires a steadier grip, so 'lift' probability falls and 'move camera' (to get a better view first) rises.

What the Lectures Emphasize

In CS224R, the lecturer stresses that a policy is not a fixed plan. It is a learned conditional rule you can adapt and compose. A policy can be conditioned on task information—the same policy network handles multiple tasks by feeding in a task descriptor alongside the state. Policies can also be stacked: one high-level policy decides which skill to invoke, and each skill is a lower-level policy. The key insight is that a policy generates the data you use to learn—what you observe and what rewards you get depend on which actions the policy chose—so the policy is the center of the learning loop.

Common Misunderstanding

People often imagine a policy as a fixed sequence of steps: 'First do A, then do B, then stop.' In reality, modern policies are stochastic (they assign probabilities, not certainties) and reactive (they adjust to the situation they see). The same policy can be run many times and produce different action sequences each time because it samples actions probabilistically.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In papers and systems, look for the pattern of 'choosing actions from observations.' The policy may be called a controller, actor, planner, behavior model, or decoder, but the core is the same: a learned rule mapping state or context to action choices. When you see a system that can adapt its behavior to new situations, or compose short behaviors into longer tasks, or condition on a goal or task descriptor, you are seeing a policy at work.

Connected Concepts

In Deep RL (CS224R), the policy is the object you optimize using policy gradients or actor-critic methods. In Diffusion/Vision (CME296), a diffusion model can be understood as sampling from a learned conditional distribution—the connection is that both learn a conditional probability distribution over actions or denoising steps. In Transformers/LLMs (CME295), the transformer's output layer is a policy over which token to generate next—it maps the sequence context to a probability distribution over vocabulary. All three share the pattern: learn to map observations to action choices under uncertainty.
