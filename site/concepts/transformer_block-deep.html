The Transformer Block — how layers sharpen a guess, one step at a time

A single pass of attention mixes information once, but reading a word in context means going back and forth, fitting it into the whole picture, updating your guess as you learn more. The transformer solves this by stacking a tiny unit — attention to gather information, then a small feed-forward step to process each word — over and over, a dozen times or more. Each repetition refines the guess. We show this on a real run: the token the model will finally choose starts buried deep in the ranking of 50,257 words, and climbs toward the top as it passes through the layers, until by the end it is the clear winner. Here is the core idea of every large language model, the way depth and iteration are engineered together, and why layering actually works.

1 · The problem — one pass is not enough

A single round of attention can gather information. But understanding a sentence is not a one-pass job. Consider 'Alice met Bob at the bank near the river because it was on her way.' The word 'it' needs to refer back to 'bank', but only after the word 'river' (which could also be the antecedent) appears and is ruled out by context — the 'bank' we mean is not beside a river, it is a financial institution. Resolving this takes multiple rounds of thought: look, combine, notice new constraints, look again, refine the guess.

So the core need is: how can a model revisit and refine its understanding over multiple rounds instead of making one bet at the start?

2 · The reframe — stack the block, repeat it, let each layer build on the last

The trick is to build a small unit — call it a block — that does one round of understand better work, then stack it over and over. Each block has two parts. First, attention: gather information by looking back at other words and deciding which ones matter for this word right now. Second, a feed-forward step: a tiny neural network, applied separately to each word, that has room to digest and process what this word has learned so far. The block takes a set of word representations, runs them through attention (mixing information across the sequence), then through the feed-forward layer (processing each word's updated knowledge), and outputs refined representations.

Now stack a dozen of these blocks, one after another. The first block makes a rough guess; the second refines it with a second look; the third refines the refinement. Each block inherits what the previous one learned, treats it as a fresh starting point, and adds another layer of depth. Depth — stacking these blocks — is the transformer's way of engineering multiple rounds of reasoning into a single forward pass through the model.

The hidden quantity is the running summary each token carries — every block reads that summary, mixes in a little context and computation, and writes it back, so meaning accumulates layer by layer.

3 · Seen on a real run — the rank climbs as layers refine

We ran the actual GPT-2 (a real pretrained model with 124 million learned numbers) on a sentence and tracked a single question: where does the token the model will finally choose sit in the ranking of all 50,257 possible words after each of the 12 layers? This is called the logit lens — it lets us watch the model's guess sharpen layer by layer.

The discovered fact: the correct token starts at rank 32,406 — deeply buried among 50,257 possible words — after the first layer. By layer 6 it has climbed to rank 2. By layer 12, the final layer, it is ranked 1, the clear winner. This is not a flat convergence; the model gets progressively more confident, and each layer does real work sharpening the guess.

4 · Why layering sharpens the guess — surface patterns before deep meaning

The insight is that early layers and later layers work on different kinds of problems. The first layers can extract simple features: this word rhymes with that one, this word often follows that one, this is a noun. Later layers have access to all that extracted information and can compose it into higher meanings: given that it is a noun, and given that this other token is a verb describing it, what is the full concept? Early layers handle the surface; later layers assemble meaning.

Each layer refines because it inherits all the information the previous layer extracted — the attention patterns, the refined word representations — and adds one more pass of looking and thinking. The guess climbs from rank 32,406 to rank 1 because each layer does one more pass of gathering (attention) and processing (feed-forward), each one ruling out more wrong options and promoting the right one. The layering is not redundant; it is how the model converges to the right answer through iteration.

5 · The one real weakness — depth costs compute and can be hard to train

More layers mean more computation — twelve layers means twelve passes over the sequence, which is why a 12-layer model costs roughly twice as much as a 6-layer one. Even more problematic, deep networks can be unstable during training: gradients can shrink or explode as they flow backward through many layers, and the signal can get lost. This is why all real transformer models use residual connections (each layer adds to what came before instead of replacing it) and normalization (stabilizing the numerical scale between layers) — these tricks exist specifically to keep deep stacks trainable. And even with these tricks, adding layers gives diminishing returns: going from 12 to 24 layers helps less than going from 1 to 12.

6 · The surprise — the same block, repeated and scaled, is almost all of modern AI

This one block — attention plus a small feed-forward step — repeated a dozen times (or 96 times in the case of much larger models like GPT-4), with careful residual connections and normalization to keep it stable, is the entire internal design of the transformer. The same architecture, unchanged, powers attention, which does the information gathering. The block takes embeddings as input (words turned into vectors). Position information comes from positional encoding. Swap the word tokens for image patches, and the same block is the core of vision transformers, which process images. Scale it from 124 million numbers to billions and train it on pretraining, and you get a state-of-the-art language model. This single, repeated, stacked unit is the foundation of nearly every large model reshaping AI in the last five years — learn how it works and you have learned the shape of the whole field.

7 · Where it is used in practice — Stack tiny units to refine understanding through layers

The transformer block is the core repeated unit in nearly all large language models and vision systems, including GPT models, Gemini, Claude, and ViT-based image classifiers. Chat assistants use stacked blocks to process and refine token representations. Image understanding models stack the same block architecture, treating image patches as sequences just as they treat word tokens.

Each block performs two operations in sequence: first, attention gathers information by allowing each token to look back at all other tokens and decide which ones are relevant, mixing their information together. Second, a small feed-forward neural network processes the mixed information separately for each token. Multiple blocks are stacked—often a dozen or more—so that early blocks extract simple patterns and later blocks combine those patterns into higher-level meaning. Each block inherits the refined representations from the block before it, allowing iterative refinement.

Depth provides expressive power but also computational cost: a model with twice as many blocks needs roughly twice the computation, so practitioners must balance how much refinement is worth the added expense.
