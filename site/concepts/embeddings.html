Embeddings

Taking a word (or a piece of an image, or a game state) and turning it into a list of numbers that sits at a specific point in a high-dimensional space. Similar words end up near each other in that space; different words are far apart.

What real-world problem is this about?

You walk into a library where every book has only a number on its spine: 4129, 983, 7634. These numbers are arbitrary labels—you cannot tell if book 4129 is science fiction and 983 is a cookbook, or whether 4129 and 7634 are related. You have no way to find books like the one you just read, and no way to know which books often go on the same shelf.

Why does this problem exist?

When computers process words, pictures, or actions, they start with arbitrary labels like 4129 or 'cat.' But to learn patterns—which words appear together, which game actions lead to winning, which images look similar—the computer must arrange these items in a space where closeness has meaning. Without that, the computer cannot smooth out its guesses when it gets feedback. It treats every item as completely separate.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

An embedding table is a huge grid of numbers. Each row holds a list of numbers (usually 768 of them) that represents one word. If you want the embedding for word 4129, you grab row 4129 from the table. The distance between two words is calculated by taking their number lists and multiplying them element-by-element and adding up the results—this is called a dot product. If the dot product is large, the words point in the same direction and are 'close.' Training nudges all these numbers so that words that appear together in sentences end up with large dot products, pulling their representations toward each other.

Why is this concept important?

Once words live in a space as lists of numbers, the computer can ask 'which words are closest to each other?' by computing dot products. It can find similar words for retrieval. It can average several word-embeddings to understand a phrase. It can watch how numbers change during learning to understand what the model figured out. Every major operation that follows—comparing context, making predictions, finding relevant chunks—relies on having words as organized positions in space rather than bare labels.

What breaks without it?

Without embeddings, the computer sees only labels: 4129, 983, 2754. There is no connection between them. If the computer learns something about word 4129, it cannot apply that knowledge to a similar word. Each label stands alone. The computer cannot share patterns between similar inputs or build the foundation for tasks like translation, question-answering, or playing games.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner asks: why train an embedding table at all? Why not just use the number itself (4129 stays 4129), or make a one-hot vector—a list where one position has a 1 and all others are 0 (so word 4129 gets one specific position marked 1)? That is simpler and does not require training.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Using raw numbers fails because 4129 and 983 are arbitrary—the computer learns nothing from them being close or far. One-hot vectors are worse: the one-hot for 'cat' looks completely unrelated to the one-hot for 'kitten' (they share no 1s). There is no geometry. The computer has no mechanism to notice that similar words should have similar representations. As training feedback arrives, related words do not learn to cluster together. Only a real learned table, trained by trial and error, can build a space where closeness reflects what the computer discovered from data.

The Mathematical Object

The embedding table is a real matrix you can see. If your vocabulary has 50,000 words and you pick 768 as the size of each word's number list, the table is 50,000 rows by 768 columns of real numbers. During training, feedback flows backward and nudges every single number in this table. You can shrink it to two dimensions and plot each word as a point on paper; related words cluster visibly. You can pull out one row (one word's numbers) and feed it to the next operation, or analyze it to see what the model learned.

The Operation

To use embeddings: (1) Break the input text into word IDs like [4129, 2754, 983]. (2) Look up each ID as a row in the embedding table to get its list of numbers. (3) Pass these number-lists to the next stage (a mechanism that compares context, a classifier, or a search module). During training, feedback from the model's final answer flows backward, gradually changing the embedding table numbers so that words appearing in similar contexts end up with similar number-lists.

Worked Mini-Example

Say your vocabulary is three words: 'cat' (ID 4129), 'dog' (ID 983), 'meow' (ID 7634). The embedding table starts with random numbers for each. During training, the model reads sentences like 'cat meow' and 'dog bark.' The context-comparing mechanism learns that 'cat' and 'meow' often go together, so feedback pushes their number-lists closer. Same for 'dog' and 'bark.' After training, the dot product of 'cat' and 'meow' is large (they are close), while 'cat' and 'dog' have moderate similarity (both animals, some overlap). Shrink the 768 numbers to two dimensions for a picture: 'cat' and 'dog' cluster together (animals), while 'meow' and 'bark' cluster separately (actions), showing what the model learned.

What the Lectures Emphasize

In CME295 (Transformers), the lecture explains that embeddings are the first numerical form that text takes: text becomes words (tokens), and words become number-lists before any transformer machinery runs. BERT shows embeddings are rich enough to solve downstream tasks like classification. In retrieval-augmented-generation, embeddings are the lookup mechanism: to find relevant context, turn the question into a number-list and pull back chunks with similar number-lists (measured by dot product). Across all three courses, embeddings reveal the pattern: anything discrete (a word, an image tile, a game state) must first become a position in learned space so the learning algorithms can operate.

Common Misunderstanding

People often think an embedding somehow contains a human-readable definition—if you could read the 768 numbers, you would see 'is_animate=0.8, is_noun=0.9.' In truth, embeddings are bundles of learned numbers. Two words ending up close just means the training process pushed them together because they were useful in the same computational contexts, not because they share one obvious property. Most individual numbers do not have interpretable meaning on their own.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In research papers, spot embeddings when discrete items (words, image patches, game states, actions, documents) are first turned into number-lists in a learned space before the main algorithm runs. The term might be 'encoding,' 'representation,' 'learned codes,' or 'expansion into learned space.' The mark is always the same: discrete item → look it up in a learned table → continuous number-list → send to next algorithm. Look for phrases like 'we embed the query and documents' or 'state representation is learned end-to-end.'

Connected Concepts

Embeddings are how all three courses turn discrete identity into continuous geometry. In CME295 (language), embeddings follow tokenization and feed into context-comparison. In CME296 (diffusion), moving discrete object classes into a continuous space mirrors the embedding idea. In CS224R (reinforcement learning), state and action embeddings let the value estimator and strategy network generalize across similar situations. The common thread: turn identity into position so learning can move things toward useful locations.
