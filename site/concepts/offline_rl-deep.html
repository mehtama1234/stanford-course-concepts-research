Offline RL — learning from a fixed dataset when you cannot experiment

In medicine, autonomous driving, or any costly domain, you cannot let an agent experiment freely. You have a log — maybe years of past human decisions, past treatment outcomes, past driving videos — and you need to learn a good policy from that alone, with no new interaction. That is the offline reinforcement learning problem. Here is the core challenge: standard learning methods assume you can try anything and learn from mistakes, but a fixed log shows you only a slice of the world. Some situations and actions never appear in the log. An optimistic learner will hallucinate high values for those unseen actions and chase them into disaster. Here is how the trap works, a real run showing naive learning fail where a conservative method succeeds, why the unseen is the fatal flaw, the real ceiling you hit, and where this exact setting reappears in modern AI.

1 · The problem — the log is biased and incomplete

In the real world, you rarely get to experiment freely. In a hospital, giving a new drug to a patient and watching what happens is costly and risky. In a self-driving car, trial-and-error crashes are unacceptable. In business, you have historical data — logs of decisions made by people or old systems — and you must learn from it. The log is what it is: it shows you some situations and some actions, maybe heavy in one region of the world and sparse in another, maybe skewed toward safer or easier choices. It does not show you what happens if you try something no one in the log ever tried. And yet you need to build a policy — a rule that decides what to do — that is better, or at least as good, as the behavior that generated the log.

The trap is this: the log is both your only teacher and your only source of truth, yet it is guaranteed to be incomplete. Some combinations of situation and action never occur. A naive learning method — one that works fine when you can experiment — will make up confident guesses about what would happen in those unseen cases. It will chase those guesses. And it will fail, often catastrophically.

2 · The reframe — stay within the log, or pay the price

Here is the key shift in thinking. When you have a live world and can experiment, you learn by trying things and observing the results. When you have only a log, you must flip the problem: treat the log as a dataset, like you would train a classifier on labeled photos. But recognize that your dataset is not a random sample of the world — it is heavily biased toward whatever behavior generated it. Anywhere the log is silent, your estimates are hallucinations.

The safest response is to stay close to the log. If an action never appeared in a given situation, do not trust any value estimate for it — or, better, never even consider it. If you only saw three of ten possible actions in a situation, focus on those three. A method that respects this boundary, staying within the support of the data, will never chase imaginary values into disaster. It might not discover anything better than the log's original behavior — there is a real ceiling — but it will not crash through it either.

3 · Seen on a real run — naive optimism fails, conservative learning finds the goal

We trained a small gridworld with a 6 by 6 grid, a goal at one corner, and a cost of 1 for each step. The optimal path is 10 steps. We then created a biased log by running a random agent that preferentially took certain actions — in total, only 65 of the possible 144 state-action pairs ever appeared in the log. Now we had a fixed dataset: incomplete and skewed, just like a real human driving log or medical dataset.

We then trained two learners on that fixed log, with no new interaction. The first was a copy of standard online Q-learning — a method that works well when you can try new things. The second was a conservative method designed for offline settings, one that stays within the actions and state-action pairs the log showed.

The discovered fact: with only 65 of 144 state-action pairs ever logged, naive Q-learning never reaches the goal, while a conservative method that stays within the logged actions finds the optimal 10-step path. The naive method learned optimistic value estimates for the 79 unseen state-action pairs. It treated them as high-value moves. It committed to them. It crashed. The conservative method said: I only trust what I see. It stayed on the 65 state-action islands in the log, and from those stepping stones alone it navigated to the goal.

4 · Why the unseen is a trap — standard learning trusts what it has not seen

That optimism is the engine of exploration in a live world — it drives the agent to try new things and discover better policies than the log. But in offline learning, there is no live world. A state-action pair you have never seen is not an unexplored opportunity; it is blind spot. You have zero evidence about what it does. Standard Q-learning will nonetheless assign it an optimistic value, often very high, because you have not seen a failure. The agent acts on that hallucination. It commits to an unseen path. And because the log never showed what happens there, you have no mechanism to correct the mistake.

5 · The one real weakness — you cannot exceed the log's ceiling

The conservative solution — stay within the data — solves the hallucination problem completely. You will not crash into imaginary cliffs. But there is a hard trade-off: you cannot discover anything better than the behavior that generated the log. If the log was collected by a mediocre or biased agent, you are stuck improving a mediocre policy. You cannot invent new actions or combinations the log never tried. The best you can hope for is to extract every ounce of value from the logged behavior and avoid its mistakes. In medicine, you might decide: the doctors in my log were pretty good; let me learn their policy exactly and apply it consistently. But you will never discover a novel treatment no doctor in the log ever prescribed. In driving, you can learn from human trajectories, but you cannot discover driving moves humans never recorded. The ceiling is real.
