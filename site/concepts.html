Tokenization

Converting text into a list of numbered pieces that a machine can process.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you want to teach a foreigner English by showing them a book, but they can only learn 10,000 chunks at a time. If you teach them individual letters, they'll need to learn rules for combining them forever. If you teach them whole words, they'll trip over 'unbelievably' every time they see it because you taught them only 'believe'. You need chunks that reuse across words.
First-Principles Reason
Computers cannot digest raw text the way you read it. They need to turn language into a sequence of numbers. But language is endless—new words, names, and spellings arrive constantly. So you must choose a fixed vocabulary (usually 30,000–50,000 items) that works as a universal building set. That vocabulary is your tokenizer.
Math Underneath
A tokenizer is a lookup table: you map each possible chunk of text to an integer ID. For example, 'hello' might map to 42, 'world' to 999. When you feed the sentence 'hello world' to a transformer, it becomes the sequence [42, 999]. The genius is in choosing the chunks. Most modern tokenizers split text into subwords (morphemes and common pieces), so 'unbelievably' breaks into ['un', 'believ', 'able', 'ly']. Each piece gets an ID: maybe 201, 456, 78, 23. Now the model sees 'unbelievably' as [201, 456, 78, 23], and 'believe' becomes [456] plus context—the same piece 201 reappears in 'unable', 'unlock', etc.

Embeddings

A table of learned numbers that assigns every token, patch, or state a unique location in a multidimensional space, where distance and direction carry meaning.

Everyday Problem
You walk into a library and find two books labeled only by number: 4129 and 983. The numbers tell you nothing about whether they are novels or cookbooks, whether one is related to the other, or what to read next. You need a system that captures what each book is actually about.
First-Principles Reason
When a computer system processes discrete items (words, images, actions), it starts with arbitrary labels. To learn useful relationships—which words appear together, which states lead to reward, which images show similar objects—the system must place these items in a space where similarity becomes measurable by geometry. Otherwise, the system cannot smoothly adjust what it knows when feedback arrives.
Math Underneath
An embedding is a learned function that maps each discrete item (like a word) to a vector, a list of numbers. If you have an embedding table E with dimensions (vocabulary_size, embedding_dimension), and you want the vector for word w, you look up row w in that table: v_w = E[w]. Training adjusts all the numbers in E so that vectors for related items end up close to each other. Closeness is measured by dot product: v_w · v_x = sum of (element_i in v_w times element_i in v_x). Large dot product means the vectors point in similar directions, capturing semantic or functional similarity learned from data.

Position And Order

A numerical tag attached to each input that tells the model which slot in the sequence or image it occupies.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you're listening to a friend describe a scene, but they rattle off words in a jumbled order: "dog bites man" versus "man bites dog." Same words, opposite meaning. The order matters. Now imagine a listener who hears all the words at the same time, as a cloud with no internal sequence. That listener has no built-in way to know which word came first unless you mark each one with a position tag.
First-Principles Reason
Transformers work by comparing any token to any other token directly in parallel, unlike older systems that processed tokens one-by-one and thus learned sequence order naturally. Because attention links bypass the sequential order, the model has no automatic way to know where each token sits in the sequence. Something must explicitly label position, or the model loses that information entirely.
Math Underneath
A position embedding is a vector (a list of numbers) assigned to position k. The simplest version uses sinusoidal functions: embed(k) = [sin(k/10000^0), cos(k/10000^0), sin(k/10000^2), cos(k/10000^2), ...]. Each dimension oscillates at a different frequency—low frequencies change slowly (far-away positions look different), high frequencies change quickly (nearby positions stay similar). Adding this vector to the token's embedding lets attention patterns attend based on distance, because the position signal is now mixed into the data the model compares.

Attention

A mechanism where each piece of information learns to focus on a weighted blend of other pieces, based on what they contain.

Everyday Problem
You're at a party full of conversations. To understand what your friend just said, you can't equally weight every background noise—you need to focus harder on voices that are speaking to your situation, softer on distant chatter. Your brain solves this by guessing what's relevant and listening to those signals more.
First-Principles Reason
When information flows through a system, not every input piece matters equally for every output decision. A mechanism that can learn to filter, rank, and blend based on content lets the system adapt its focus to what actually matters in each situation, rather than using a rigid rule.
Math Underneath
Each item creates three things: a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I contain?), and a value (what information should I contribute?). The query gets compared to every key using dot-product multiplication (which is larger when two vectors point in similar directions). These match-scores then get normalized using a softmax function (which turns raw scores into weights that sum to one, making high scores much larger than low ones). Finally, a weighted average of all values is computed using those weights. The compact formula softmax((Q × K-transpose) / √(dimension)) × V captures this: numerator is the query-key comparisons, the division by √dimension prevents scores from exploding, softmax normalizes, and multiplying by V applies those weights to the stored information.

Transformer Block

A repeating layer that lets information flow between positions, updates each position independently, and preserves old state while adding new updates.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you're reading a long email on a phone screen that shows only one sentence at a time. You glance at "The bank is closed on Sundays." To understand "bank," you need to scroll back to see "The river ran past the old bank." But your brain is finite—you can't hold all context at once. You need a system that repeatedly focuses on relevant parts, updates your understanding, and carries important ideas forward to the next thought.
First-Principles Reason
Understanding complex text or images requires multiple passes. On pass one, you might spot that "bank" appears near "river." On pass two, you notice grammar ties it to a verb. On pass three, you connect it to earlier context about geography. A single lookup doesn't build this layered meaning. You need a reusable recipe: focus on dependencies, reshape information based on that focus, preserve what you learned, and repeat. That's why layers stack.
Math Underneath
A transformer block has four moving parts. First, multi-head attention mixes information: for each position, compute how similar it is to every other position (dot-product similarity, scaled by a constant to keep numbers stable), then use those similarity scores as weights to average all the information at those positions. The scaling prevents any single similarity from dominating. Second, a feed-forward network (a small nonlinear function applied to each position separately) reshapes the mixed information—like adding learned color to a grayscale image. Third, residual connections (adding the input to the output of each step) preserve old state so information doesn't wash away through layers. Fourth, layer normalization (centering and scaling activations) keeps numbers in a comfortable range, which makes training stable. These four pieces fit together: mix → reshape → carry old state → keep numbers sane.

Pretraining

Learning broad patterns from massive amounts of unlabeled data before teaching the model to do anything specific.

Everyday Problem
You want to teach someone a skill, but good instructors are rare and expensive. However, raw examples of the skill in action are everywhere—books, videos, conversations. Someone who reads thousands of books absorbs patterns long before being taught explicitly.
First-Principles Reason
A learner faces two constraints: good labeled data is rare, but patterns in raw data are free. Any system smart enough to extract patterns from raw examples before seeing labeled ones will need less labeled data later. That's a practical necessity, not a choice.
Math Underneath
The model learns by reducing a loss—a number that measures how wrong or surprised the model was about what came next. When you predict the next word in a sentence and it's wrong, the loss is high; when you're right, it's low. The model adjusts its internal knobs (weights) to make this number smaller. Mathematically, you compute the average loss over millions of examples, then use calculus to find which direction each knob should turn. The reason this works is that patterns in language show up repeatedly—if you practice predicting 'next word' across billions of examples, you absorb structure like grammar, facts, and reasoning shortcuts without anyone telling you what they are.

Fine-Tuning And Preference Learning

Taking a general model and adjusting its weights so it reliably does what you actually need it to do.

Everyday Problem
A chess engine trained on millions of game records is a great predictor of legal moves, but doesn't play to win. A student who reads textbooks thoroughly can recite facts but struggles with exam questions. They need more than statistical frequency—they need alignment with what works.
First-Principles Reason
When you train a model on raw data, it learns patterns in that data—what words appear together, what moves are legal, what facts exist. That is not the same as learning what you want. You need a second phase: using feedback (human judgments, rewards, or correct answers for your specific task) to reshape the model's probabilities so it does what matters to you, not just what's common in the original data.
Math Underneath
Training a model means adjusting its weights (the numbers inside) to minimize a loss function, a measure of prediction error. Fine-tuning keeps the same architecture but changes the loss: instead of matching 'next token is X' across billions of documents, you match 'next token is Y for this task' on focused, curated examples. The math is identical—gradient descent (small nudges downhill in error space)—but the goal changes from 'replicate the training set' to 'get task examples right'. Preference learning adds a comparison: given two outputs for the same input, make the better one more likely and the worse one less likely, encoding human judgment as probabilities.

Reasoning Traces

Writing out intermediate steps before jumping to a final answer, so earlier work stays visible for later reasoning.

Everyday Problem
You're doing your taxes. If you leap straight to the total deduction, you'll probably miss something. But if you write down each category first—medical, charity, work expenses—and sum them, you catch mistakes. The paper trail lets you backtrack.
First-Principles Reason
The human brain can't hold seven years of tax records in working memory at once. We externalize onto paper. Similarly, a language model has finite context attention. Writing intermediate results into the token sequence lets the model refer back to earlier steps instead of guessing. It trades immediate memory for a longer record.
Math Underneath
The model generates a token sequence longer than the direct answer: T = [input, step1, step2, ..., stepN, final_answer]. Each token depends on all previous tokens in the sequence via the transformer's attention mechanism (a learned pattern-matching system). Attention computes which earlier tokens are relevant to predict the next token. When step2 attends back to step1, that earlier work shapes the prediction. The extra tokens cost computation time but add more moments for the model to incorporate prior work into what comes next.

Agents, Retrieval, And Tools

A loop where a language model takes an action, sees the result, then decides what to do next.

Everyday Problem
You ask a librarian a question about a book published yesterday. The librarian cannot answer from memory alone. She has to walk to the shelf, check the catalog, pull down a reference, read the new information, then come back and answer you. Her knowledge is not enough; she needs to act and observe.
First-Principles Reason
A language model's weights contain facts frozen at training time. Real tasks demand current weather, database lookups, running code, or changing the world. No weights can store 'today's date' or 'the stock price right now.' So the model must step outside itself, observe, and adjust. This is not a choice — it is a logical necessity.
Math Underneath
A model maintains a state (what it knows so far). At step t, it chooses an action a_t (a tool to call or query to send) based on the state. The action produces an observation o_t (the result). The state updates: state_{t+1} = state_t + a_t + o_t. The model then uses the new state to choose the next action a_{t+1}. This feedback loop, not a single forward pass, drives the system. Each action narrows the space of possible next states, creating a search through possibilities.

Evaluation

A structured test that reveals what a model actually does — and what it fails to do — across different situations.

Everyday Problem
You hire a tutor who seems brilliant on math problems from the textbook. Only when your child takes a real test do you discover the tutor has memorized only that book. The tutor looks competent within a narrow slice but fails everywhere else. You needed a test that covered the full range of problems your child would actually face.
First-Principles Reason
Any claim about ability is invisible until you check it against reality. If you only watch the model in situations you chose, you get a skewed picture — like testing a weatherman only on sunny days. You must design tests that probe the places where failure is most likely. This forces you to think carefully: what does success actually mean for this job? What are the edge cases where things break?
Math Underneath
A metric is a function that takes behaviors (the model's outputs) and compresses them into numbers or tables you can compare. For example, accuracy counts correct answers: metric(outputs) = (number correct) / (total). But a single number discards details. A chatbot might score 85% accurate on math but fail harder on logic puzzles; accuracy alone hides this. Better metrics are often multi-dimensional — separate tables for math, writing, reasoning. The key insight: every number you report means you have thrown away other information. The hidden information is often as important as the reported score.

Policy

A rule that maps what an agent observes to what it chooses to do.

Everyday Problem
You are arranging a dinner party. Guests arrive tired or energized based on the route they took, the people they talk to, and how much help you ask them to give. Each choice you make now—who sits where, what you ask someone to do—changes the mood and events that come next. You need a consistent way to decide what to do based on what you're seeing, not a different strategy for every possible table arrangement.
First-Principles Reason
When an action causes the world to change, you can't choose blindly or use the same fixed answer every time. The situation keeps shifting. You need a mapping—a rule that looks at the current state and says what to do next. Without it, there's no way to connect observation to choice or to improve behavior systematically.
Math Underneath
A policy is written as π(a|s): the probability of choosing action a given the situation s. Think of it as a function—you feed in what you see (s), and it outputs the odds of each possible action (a). If you're in situation s1, the policy might say there's a 60% chance to do action 1 and 40% chance to do action 2. Why odds instead of a single answer? Because the best move often depends on hidden details you can't see—so expressing uncertainty (a probability distribution, meaning the odds of each choice) lets the agent hedge and explore wisely. The mathematical form π(a|s) is just a conditional probability: given s is true, what is the likelihood of a?

Reward

A number that tells a system which futures to make more likely.

Everyday Problem
You're teaching a dog a new trick. The dog tries something. Did it help or hurt? Without knowing that, the dog can't learn which behaviors to repeat. You need a way to say "yes, that was better" or "no, try again."
First-Principles Reason
Any learning system facing a choice needs to know which choice worked. Reward is the answer to that question—a number that marks outcomes as better or worse. Without it, the system collects experience but has no direction to improve.
Math Underneath
Reward usually measures a single step. Return (often written R) sums rewards across multiple steps into one total. To value an action fairly, look at all the downstream consequences, not just the immediate result. A detour might cost you now but save you later. The formula R = r₀ + r₁ + r₂ + ... adds up each step's reward (rₜ) forward in time. Often future rewards matter less than immediate ones—a coffee today beats a coffee a year from now—so we discount: R = r₀ + γ·r₁ + γ²·r₂ + ..., where γ (gamma, between 0 and 1) shrinks distant rewards. This forces the system to prioritize.

Credit Assignment

Tracing backward through time to figure out which specific earlier decision caused a later outcome, good or bad.

Everyday Problem
You bake a cake that comes out burnt. The mistake wasn't adding the wrong ingredient five minutes ago—it was setting the oven temperature wrong an hour before. By the time you see the burnt cake, the real culprit is buried in history. How do you know which step to fix next time?
First-Principles Reason
Outcomes have memory. When something happens now, it's the result of a chain of choices stretching back in time. If you only blame or praise the last step, you miss the real causes. Any system that learns from delayed feedback must have a way to ask: which earlier decision actually mattered?
Math Underneath
We use a discount factor—a number slightly less than 1, like 0.99—that we apply more heavily to rewards far in the future and less heavily to rewards soon. The return (total sum of future rewards) is calculated as: R_t = r_t + γ·r_{t+1} + γ²·r_{t+2} + ..., where r is the immediate reward at each step, γ (gamma) is the discount factor, and the power of γ grows with each step into the future. This formula weights recent rewards more (because γ^0 = 1, γ^1 = 0.99, γ^2 = 0.9801...). The discount factor serves two purposes: it mathematically shrinks distant rewards so learning focuses on nearby causes, and it reflects real-world intuition—a reward today matters more than a reward next year. Without discounting, we'd sum infinite rewards and get infinity; with it, we get a finite number we can actually learn from.

Policy Gradient

A recipe for tuning your action strategy by making the choices that worked better more likely next time.

Everyday Problem
You're learning to cook, but no one tells you which moves are 'right'—you only taste the result. How do you figure out which ingredients and techniques to favor, when you have to learn from trying things and seeing what happens?
First-Principles Reason
If you can sample actions and see outcomes, the most direct path to improvement is to shift probabilities: boost the odds of actions that led to good outcomes, shrink the odds of actions from bad ones. You're not guessing the right action; you're learning which choices your policy favors.
Math Underneath
The gradient (direction of steepest climb) of the log probability of an action, weighted by how much better its outcome was than average. In symbols: push the probability of action a in state s upward by an amount proportional to (return - baseline), where 'log probability' means taking the log of how likely your policy thinks this action is, 'return' is the sum of future rewards, and 'baseline' is your policy's expected return, so you only reward actions that beat the benchmark.

Actor-Critic

A learner made of two parts: one that picks actions, one that evaluates how good they turned out.

Everyday Problem
You're learning to cook. After trying a new recipe, you ask: 'Was that actually good, or did I just get lucky with ingredients today?' Comparing against only your memory of past meals is noisy—some were memorable for wrong reasons. You need a stable reference point to learn what really helps.
First-Principles Reason
When you choose an action and see what happens, two separate problems appear: figuring out which action to try next, and measuring whether this action was actually smart. These problems interfere if you solve them together. Separating them means the measurement system stays steady while the chooser improves, and the chooser gets clearer feedback.
Math Underneath
The actor is a function (the policy) that takes the current situation and outputs an action choice, updated by moving in the direction that increases expected future reward. The critic is a separate function (the value function) that takes the current situation and outputs a prediction of total future reward from here, updated to match actual outcomes. The key insight: subtract the critic's prediction from actual reward to get the advantage (the part of reward this action specifically earned, not just being in a good situation). The actor then learns from this advantage signal, which has less noise than raw reward because it removes the baseline of 'how good is this state anyway?'

Q-Learning

A method to estimate, for each state-action pair, what total reward you expect if you take that action now and then do your best from then on.

Everyday Problem
You're at a restaurant deciding between the familiar dish and an unknown special. The special might be delicious tomorrow and lead to a favorite memory, or mediocre today with no future benefit. But you must choose right now without yet knowing how it will unfold.
First-Principles Reason
Any smart decision-maker facing delayed consequences must do this: you can't wait to see the final outcome before choosing, so you need a way to *guess* what the future will be worth. The only way to make a guess is to separate the immediate reward from the delayed reward—one you know now, one you estimate. Then add them up.
Math Underneath
The idea is written as Q(s, a) = E[r + γ·max Q(s', a')]. Here, s is your current situation (state), a is your choice (action), r is the reward you get right away, γ (gamma, a number between 0 and 1) is how much you care about the future relative to now, s' is the next situation, and max Q(s', a') means the best estimated future reward you expect. The sum r + γ·max Q(s', a') is a guess: today's immediate payoff plus the discounted estimate of what comes next. You keep revising this guess based on real outcomes.

Offline Reinforcement Learning

Learning to make decisions from a fixed historical record of past actions and their outcomes, without trying new actions yourself.

Everyday Problem
A hospital administrator notices her doctors make decisions recorded in patient charts going back ten years. She wants to train a medical assistant to learn from those charts alone, but can't let it experiment on live patients. The assistant has only what history provides—no freedom to try a risky treatment just to see what happens.
First-Principles Reason
When you learn from a fixed dataset, you see the world only through the lens of choices someone else already made. You lack proof that actions outside that slice even exist or how they end. Any system trying to learn from history alone must solve this visibility gap: it cannot trust its own guesses about unseen territory.
Math Underneath
The core mathematics tracks what we call distribution shift—the mismatch between actions in the data and actions the learned policy wants to take. Suppose the dataset mostly shows doctors prescribing Drug A for symptom X, and rarely Drug B. A naive learner might estimate Drug B looks amazing because the few times it was tried it worked—but that few instances could be flukes, contaminated by unmeasured differences in patient state. Conservative methods address this by either penalizing actions the dataset rarely showed (shrinking their estimated value), or constraining the learned policy to stay close to patterns the data actually contains. Mathematically: if action *a* appears in the data with frequency below some threshold, we down-weight estimates for *a* or add a penalty term to suppress choosing it.

Model-Based Reinforcement Learning

Learning a model of what comes next so you can imagine futures before acting.

Everyday Problem
You're teaching a friend to cook a new recipe. If they only learn by trial and error—actually making the dish, tasting it, adjusting—they waste ingredients and time. If they could imagine what the dish would taste like before they serve it, they'd waste far less.
First-Principles Reason
In any uncertain world, acting blindly means you crash into mistakes. But if you can build a mental model of how the world responds to your actions, you can think ahead: 'If I do X, that leads to Y, which is bad.' Now you don't need to actually do X to learn that lesson.
Math Underneath
A dynamics model is a function that takes two inputs—the current state (what the world looks like now) and an action (what you choose to do)—and outputs the next state (what the world will look like). Mathematically, it's usually written s_{t+1} = f(s_t, a_t) + noise, where s is state, a is action, f is the function the model learns, and the noise term (random uncertainty) accounts for the world's unpredictability. Planning then searches through imagined action sequences: 'If I pick a_1, then a_2, then a_3, what chain of states unfolds?' and picks the sequence leading to the best imagined outcome.

Exploration

Deliberately trying actions to learn something new, even when they might not work best right now.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you always order the same trusted dish at a restaurant. You know you like it. But you never discover the amazing pasta special hidden three pages into the menu. Picking your favorite every time prevents you from knowing what else exists.
First-Principles Reason
The world contains hidden information. Some actions are valuable not because they pay off immediately, but because they teach you something crucial. If you only ever do what looks best today, you'll never gather the evidence needed to find something genuinely better tomorrow.
Math Underneath
Exploration methods modify how an agent chooses actions by adding three kinds of modifications: randomness (sometimes pick randomly instead of your best guess), information bonuses (boost the score of actions you haven't tested much), or curiosity scoring (favor actions that reveal how things work). The core idea is a probability distribution—a table saying "pick this action with this likelihood." Randomness increases the chance of non-obvious actions. Information bonuses add a term that grows larger the less you know about an action; this mathematically pushes you toward trying unknowns. Curiosity scoring uses entropy or prediction error to measure surprise and rewards actions that break your expectations.

RL For LLMs

Teaching a language model to make better choices by showing it which complete outputs were good or bad.

Everyday Problem
Imagine teaching a child to cook. You can taste the final dish and say 'delicious' or 'needs salt,' but you didn't watch every hand motion. The child must figure out which of their hundred choices led to the bad taste. Feedback comes at the end, not during each step.
First-Principles Reason
A language model generates text one token at a time. If you reward only the final answer, the model must work backward: which earlier decisions led to this good ending? This is the core RL problem—mapping reward from outcome to actions spread across time.
Math Underneath
Treat token choices as actions and sequences as trajectories. A reward function scores the full response (say, +1 for correct math answer, 0 for wrong). A policy is the model's learned probability over next tokens given prior context. To improve the policy, weight actions by the reward they led to: actions in high-reward sequences get boosted. Formally, gradient updates shift log-probability of actions upward in proportion to their estimated return (future discounted reward). Why gradient? It moves probabilities in the steepest direction toward improving reward while staying near the original distribution.

Diffusion

A method for generating data by starting from pure random noise and repeatedly removing noise to create structured samples like images.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you need to describe a completely realistic painting to someone who has never seen one. You can't hand them the finished image; you can only give step-by-step instructions. But how do you break down something so intricate that you'd struggle to explain the whole thing at once?
First-Principles Reason
Any system that needs to create something complex faces a hard constraint: one giant leap from idea to finished product almost always fails. But if you break the leap into many small, local decisions—each one asking only "how is this a tiny bit cleaner?"—then each step becomes learnable and verifiable.
Math Underneath
The forward process is x_t = sqrt(1 - β_t) * x_{t-1} + sqrt(β_t) * noise, where β_t is the noise schedule (a number that grows as you add more noise) and noise is fresh randomness. This mixes the previous sample with noise by a controlled amount. The reverse process learns to undo this: at each step t, the model predicts which noise was added and subtracts it. Mathematically, you train a neural network to estimate the noise component, then subtract it: x_{t-1} = (x_t - sqrt(β_t) * predicted_noise) / sqrt(1 - β_t). The model learns this by comparing its noise predictions to the actual noise that was added during training.

Score Matching

Learning to estimate which direction points toward more likely samples by measuring how log probability changes in each direction.

Everyday Problem
You are lost in fog on a mountainside at night. You cannot see the whole landscape, but you can feel the slope beneath your feet right now. Each step, you sense whether ground rises or falls. The question: how do you learn to feel the slope correctly?
First-Principles Reason
Describing all possible shapes of a complicated distribution directly is nearly impossible. But at any one point, you can ask: which nearby direction increases probability? That local compass is far easier to learn than the entire map. Any system generating from an unknown distribution must solve this same puzzle.
Math Underneath
The score is the gradient of log probability—imagine probability as a height field, and the gradient is the direction of steepest uphill. Mathematically, score = ∇ log p(x). If p(x) is how likely a sample x is under the true data distribution, log p(x) is its logarithm, and ∇ means 'direction of steepest change in all dimensions.' The score at any point tells you: move this way and samples become more data-like. In learning, you train a neural network to predict this gradient from noisy samples, using denoising score matching—a trick where you add known noise, corrupt the sample, then teach the network to reverse that exact noise corruption.

Flow Matching

A method where a model learns a continuous path—with a direction and speed at every point—that smoothly moves random noise into real data.

Everyday Problem
You're leading someone through a dark mansion from the front entrance to a treasure room they've never seen. You can't just describe the room—you have to create a path with clear signposts at every step so they know which direction to turn and when.
First-Principles Reason
To generate complex data reliably, you need more than isolated decisions. A continuous path with learned directions at every location lets you smoothly transport simple distributions (like random noise) into complex ones (like real images), without jumping between discrete steps.
Math Underneath
A vector field is a function that assigns an arrow (direction and magnitude) to each point in space. For flow matching, we learn parameters so the field v_theta(x, t) tells us: at point x and time t, move in this direction at this speed. We train it using an L2 loss—measuring the squared difference between what our learned field predicts and what the true path requires—so the model's arrows align with the right trajectory from noise to data.

Guidance

A technique to steer a generative model toward a specific outcome—like a text prompt, label, or quality measure—while keeping outputs realistic and diverse.

Everyday Problem
You ask a friend to draw a teddy bear reading a book. They return with a beautiful bear, but it's alone—no book. The drawing is skillful and realistic, but it missed your actual request. You need a way to keep their artistry while forcing attention to the book.
First-Principles Reason
A generative model learns to make plausible things: if it sees a million bear images, it will draw bears well. But plausible is not the same as targeted. When you add a condition—a text prompt, a label, a safety rule—the model must balance: stay realistic (use what it learned about bears) while also satisfying the constraint (include a book). No single fixed rule can handle all cases. You need a mechanism that bends the generation process toward the condition at every step.
Math Underneath
During generation, the model builds an output step-by-step by predicting which direction reduces noise. Guidance works by modifying that direction using a score—a number that measures how well the current partial output satisfies your condition. If you want a bear reading a book, at each step you compute: "How much does this draft match the book-reading concept?" Then you blend the model's natural prediction with a scaled boost in the direction of higher match. Mathematically, the adjusted prediction is the original direction plus a weight times the gradient (slope) of your condition score. The weight controls the strength: too weak and you ignore your condition; too strong and outputs look unnatural because you overstrain the model away from what it learned.

Latent Space

A smaller, learned coordinate system where the model stores and manipulates the essential information in data instead of dealing with raw pixels or tokens.

Everyday Problem
Imagine your friend describes a photo they saw but only talks about the main subject, lighting, and mood—not every single pixel value. Their brain naturally compressed the image into what mattered. When a computer tries to work directly with all pixel values or token sequences, it drowns in irrelevant detail and wastes energy.
First-Principles Reason
Any system working with high-dimensional data faces a scaling wall: storing and processing every raw feature costs computation, and most of it is noise or redundancy. If you can find a smaller set of features that preserves the essential structure, the system can learn faster and generate more cheaply. This is a brute-force economic argument: do the expensive work once (learning what matters), then work in a smaller space forever.
Math Underneath
An encoder is a learnable function that takes raw data (an image, text) and maps it to a lower-dimensional vector, capturing only the essential variation. A decoder reverses this: it takes the compressed vector and reconstructs the original (or something like it). Mathematically, if x is the raw data and z is the latent representation, the encoder computes z = f(x) and the decoder computes x_reconstructed = g(z). The trick is choosing a function f such that important patterns in x are spread across the dimensions of z, while noise and surface detail are either discarded or pushed into a noise variable. This is why autoencoders work: they force the bottleneck (the z layer) to become selective about what it keeps.

Vision Transformers

A way to let different regions of an image communicate directly with each other instead of through a pipeline.

Everyday Problem
Imagine sorting photos: you spot that a face is happy by reading both the smile and the eyes, which sit far apart. If you can only look at tiny neighborhoods at a time, you'll miss the connection. The distance matters.
First-Principles Reason
Images encode meaning in spatial relationships: a face's emotion comes from eyes, nose, and mouth together; a scene's layout emerges from how objects relate to each other. A system that only patches together local clues will miss these global patterns. Any vision system solving realistic problems must let distant image parts influence each other.
Math Underneath
Start with an image and chop it into rectangular patches (say, 16×16 pixels each). Represent each patch as a vector (a list of numbers). Compute attention: for each patch, measure how much it should listen to every other patch using a dot product (multiply corresponding numbers and sum them, which measures how similar two vectors are). Then divide by the square root of the vector's size to keep the numbers stable. Pass this through softmax (a function that turns numbers into percentages that sum to 100%). Finally, multiply each other patch's vector by those percentages and sum them. This gives each patch a weighted combination of all other patches, letting distant regions influence each other in one step.

Scaling Laws

When you feed an AI system more data or computation, its performance improves in predictable patterns that you can measure and plot.

Everyday Problem
You're running a photo app that learns to recognize faces. Adding smarter algorithms helps—but so does feeding it more photos and letting it practice longer. Which should you invest in next? You need a way to predict whether spending more on computing power, collecting more photos, or both will actually improve results.
First-Principles Reason
Any learning system faces finite resources: processing power, training time, and examples to learn from. When you're bottlenecked by lack of data, throwing more compute at the problem doesn't unlock new abilities—you're stuck teaching the same lessons over and over. When you're bottlenecked by weak hardware, adding data doesn't help. The system is forced to reveal which bottleneck is tightest right now, and what happens when you loosen it.
Math Underneath
A scaling law plots a curve where the horizontal axis is some quantity you can increase (data size, computing budget, model parameters—the measurable things) and the vertical axis is performance loss (how wrong predictions are). Empirically, this curve often follows a power law: loss ∝ (1 / size^α), where α is a slope that captures how steeply performance improves. The formula tells you: if you double your resources, loss shrinks by a factor of 2^α. Why a power law? Because most learning systems don't hit walls suddenly; instead, harder examples require exponentially more data to master, and the compounding effect produces this smooth algebraic curve.

Generalization

Performing well on new cases you've never seen before, not just the ones you trained on.

Everyday Problem
You memorize every question and answer in a practice test the night before an exam. Tomorrow, the real exam has different questions. You freeze because you learned the *answers*, not how to *solve*. Your memorized knowledge won't transfer to anything new.
First-Principles Reason
Any learner faces a hard fact: the past is only a sample. You train on some examples, but real life will throw new cases at you. If your learning just memorizes accident details in the training examples, it breaks when those accidents change. The only way forward is to extract stable structure—the patterns that stay true across both old and new cases.
Math Underneath
Generalization measures the gap between training error (mistakes on examples you learned from) and test error (mistakes on completely new examples). Formally, we want to minimize test error, but we only observe training error. The gap widens when a model latches onto noise or spurious patterns unique to the training set. Why does this matter mathematically? Because the true goal—performing well in the world—is actually the test error, not training error. We use regularization (adding a penalty term to the loss function that discourages overly complex solutions) and data splitting (training on one subset, validating on another, testing on a held-out third) to estimate whether a model captures reusable structure or just fits accidents.
