Tokenization

Taking text and chopping it into numbered pieces so a computer can work with it. The computer reads these piece-numbers one at a time and learns patterns from them.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you want to teach someone English using flashcards, but they can only handle 10,000 different cards at once. If you make one card for every letter, they'll never learn the rules for combining them. If you make one card for every possible word ever written, you'll need 500,000 cards and they'll still fail on words they've never seen. You need cards that are reusable: small enough to cover everything, large enough that patterns emerge quickly.
First-Principles Reason
A computer can't read text the way your eyes do—it needs numbers. Text is infinite: new words, names, spellings show up all the time. So you pick a fixed list of chunks (usually 30,000–50,000 items) that act like LEGO bricks: you can build any sentence by stacking them. This list is called your tokenizer.
Math Underneath
A tokenizer is a table that maps each chunk of text to a number. 'Hello' might be number 42, 'world' might be 999. When you write 'hello world', the tokenizer turns it into [42, 999]. The trick is choosing smart chunks. Modern tokenizers use subword pieces: they find chunks that repeat across different words. The word 'unbelievably' becomes ['un', 'believ', 'able', 'ly'], maybe numbers [201, 456, 78, 23]. The piece 201 ('un') also appears in 'unable' and 'unlock', so the model learns that 'un' means the same thing in all three words. This reuse is the secret.

Embeddings

Taking a word (or a piece of an image, or a game state) and turning it into a list of numbers that sits at a specific point in a high-dimensional space. Similar words end up near each other in that space; different words are far apart.

Everyday Problem
You walk into a library where every book has only a number on its spine: 4129, 983, 7634. These numbers are arbitrary labels—you cannot tell if book 4129 is science fiction and 983 is a cookbook, or whether 4129 and 7634 are related. You have no way to find books like the one you just read, and no way to know which books often go on the same shelf.
First-Principles Reason
When computers process words, pictures, or actions, they start with arbitrary labels like 4129 or 'cat.' But to learn patterns—which words appear together, which game actions lead to winning, which images look similar—the computer must arrange these items in a space where closeness has meaning. Without that, the computer cannot smooth out its guesses when it gets feedback. It treats every item as completely separate.
Math Underneath
An embedding table is a huge grid of numbers. Each row holds a list of numbers (usually 768 of them) that represents one word. If you want the embedding for word 4129, you grab row 4129 from the table. The distance between two words is calculated by taking their number lists and multiplying them element-by-element and adding up the results—this is called a dot product. If the dot product is large, the words point in the same direction and are 'close.' Training nudges all these numbers so that words that appear together in sentences end up with large dot products, pulling their representations toward each other.

Position And Order

A set of numbers attached to each piece of data that tells the model what slot that piece occupies in a sequence or grid.

Everyday Problem
You hear two sentences: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog." Same three words, but they mean opposite things because the order is different. Now imagine a listener who hears all three words at once, jumbled in a cloud, with no sense of which came first. Without marking each word with its position, that listener cannot tell the two sentences apart.
First-Principles Reason
Modern AI systems called transformers work by comparing every piece of data to every other piece at the same time, all at once. This is different from older systems that read data one piece after another, where the order was automatic. When everything is compared in parallel, the system needs a way to know where each piece came from in the original sequence, or that information is lost forever.
Math Underneath
Each position gets a set of numbers (called a vector) built from repeating waves of different speeds. Position 0 might be [1.0, 0, 0.5, 0, ...], position 1 might be [0.84, 0.54, 0.38, 0.92, ...], position 2 might be [0.41, 0.91, -0.94, 0.75, ...]. The waves move at different rates: some change slowly (positions far apart look very different), some change fast (positions next to each other look similar). This pattern is then mixed together with the data the model sees.

Attention

A method that lets a system read multiple pieces of information and automatically decide which ones matter most for the current task, then blend them together in a specific way. If the system needs answer A, it pulls more from relevant information. If it needs answer B, it pulls more from different information.

Everyday Problem
You're trying to understand a sentence. Every word you read gives context, but not all words help equally. In 'The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big,' the word 'it' needs to figure out which noun it refers to—the trophy or the suitcase. Your brain does this fast: 'it' is near 'suitcase' but 'it was too big' makes sense if the trophy was the problem. You automatically weight the noun that makes sense harder than the one that doesn't.
First-Principles Reason
When a system processes text or data, each piece of output depends on multiple inputs, but not equally. A mechanism that can learn to rank inputs by relevance and combine them based on content—rather than using a rigid rule like 'always take the most recent' or 'always take an average'—lets the system adapt moment-by-moment. This solves the core problem: how do you decide what to pay attention to when the answer depends on what you're trying to compute right now?
Math Underneath
Each piece of input data gets turned into three learned representations: a query (a fingerprint asking 'what kind of information do I need?'), a key (a fingerprint saying 'what information do I contain?'), and a value (the actual information to pass forward). The query gets compared to every key using a dot product, which is a measure of similarity—larger numbers mean the fingerprints point in similar directions. These similarity scores are then normalized using a function called softmax, which converts raw scores into weights between 0 and 1 that add up to 1, making high scores much larger than low ones. Finally, each value gets multiplied by its weight and all weighted values are added together. The compact formula softmax((Q × K-transpose) / √(dimension)) × V captures this entire operation: the numerator computes how much each key matches the query, dividing by square-root of dimension keeps the scores stable, softmax turns them into weights that sum to one, and multiplying by V applies those weights to retrieve information.

Transformer Block

A repeating layer inside an AI system that lets information flow between different positions, updates each position by combining what it learns, and holds onto old information while adding new updates.

Everyday Problem
Imagine you're reading a long email on a phone screen that shows only one sentence at a time. You read "The bank is closed on Sundays." To understand what "bank" means here, you need to scroll back and see "The river ran past the old bank." Your brain is finite—you can't hold all the context at once. You need a system that repeatedly looks at the relevant parts, updates what you understand, and carries important ideas forward to the next thought. That's what the system inside an AI does when processing text.
First-Principles Reason
Understanding complex text or images requires multiple passes. On the first pass, you might spot that "bank" appears near "river." On the second pass, you notice grammar connects it to a verb. On the third pass, you link it to earlier context about geography. A single lookup doesn't build this layered meaning. You need a reusable recipe that you can repeat: look at what connects to what, reshape the information based on that connection, keep what you learned, and run again. That's why layers stack.
Math Underneath
A transformer block has four pieces that work together. First, multi-head attention mixes information: for each position, measure how similar it is to every other position using a dot product (the sum of multiplied pairs of numbers), then scale that result to keep numbers in a reasonable range, and use those similarity scores as weights to average the information at all those positions. This scaling prevents any one similarity from taking over. Second, a feed-forward network (a small learned function that runs separately at each position) reshapes the mixed information—like adding learned color to a black-and-white image. Third, residual connections (adding the original input back to the output at each step) preserve old information so it doesn't disappear when data moves through layers. Fourth, layer normalization (centering and rescaling the numbers) keeps all values in a comfortable range, which makes the training process (tuning the dials) stable and reliable. These four pieces fit together: mix → reshape → carry old state → keep numbers in range.

Pretraining

A computer learns to recognize patterns by reading through billions of examples of text, code, or images—none labeled with answers—before it's ever asked to do a specific job. Think of it like reading your entire school's library before taking a single exam.

Everyday Problem
You want to teach someone to write well. Paying experts to mark up essays is expensive and slow. But books, articles, and websites are everywhere. Someone who reads millions of words before writing learns rhythm, vocabulary, and style without anyone teaching it—they've absorbed it from examples.
First-Principles Reason
Good answers to specific questions are hard to get and expensive to label. Raw examples—text, images, video—are free and everywhere. A system that learns from raw examples first will need far fewer expensive labeled examples later. This is not a choice; it's the only way to scale.
Math Underneath
The model has millions of internal dials called weights. It makes a guess (next word, or what a fuzzy image should look like), measures how wrong it was, and turns the dials a little in whatever direction makes it less wrong on that example. It repeats this billions of times on different examples. The measure of wrongness is called loss—a single number. Calculus tells you which direction each dial should turn to shrink that loss. When you practice predicting the next word a billion times, the model absorbs structure like grammar and facts without anyone teaching it explicitly.
