Fine-Tuning And Preference Learning

Taking a general model and adjusting its weights so it reliably does what you actually need it to do.

What real-world problem is this about?

A chess engine trained on millions of game records is a great predictor of legal moves, but doesn't play to win. A student who reads textbooks thoroughly can recite facts but struggles with exam questions. They need more than statistical frequency—they need alignment with what works.

Why does this problem exist?

When you train a model on raw data, it learns patterns in that data—what words appear together, what moves are legal, what facts exist. That is not the same as learning what you want. You need a second phase: using feedback (human judgments, rewards, or correct answers for your specific task) to reshape the model's probabilities so it does what matters to you, not just what's common in the original data.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Training a model means adjusting its weights (the numbers inside) to minimize a loss function, a measure of prediction error. Fine-tuning keeps the same architecture but changes the loss: instead of matching 'next token is X' across billions of documents, you match 'next token is Y for this task' on focused, curated examples. The math is identical—gradient descent (small nudges downhill in error space)—but the goal changes from 'replicate the training set' to 'get task examples right'. Preference learning adds a comparison: given two outputs for the same input, make the better one more likely and the worse one less likely, encoding human judgment as probabilities.

Why is this concept important?

It is what separates a trained model from a usable one. Pretraining gives you broad knowledge; fine-tuning lets you steer that knowledge toward your specific needs—answering questions correctly, writing in a certain voice, refusing harmful requests, following instructions precisely.

What breaks without it?

A model trained only on raw text may be fluent and informative but refuse to answer questions, produce off-topic rambling, fall into unsafe behavior, or simply respond in a format you can't use. It becomes a fascinating pattern-matcher, not a tool.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might think: 'The model got the facts and patterns. Let me just write a simple rule: if the input asks X, output Y. Or look up the answer in a table.' This works for tiny, clean domains where you can enumerate every case by hand.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Hand-coded rules break as soon as inputs become diverse or subtle. A rule like 'always answer in one sentence' fails on questions that need three sentences. A lookup table for 'helpful responses' doesn't scale to millions of questions. You can't write a rule for tone, safety, or instruction-following that holds across all contexts. The system needs to learn what 'good' means for your task, not memorize a fixed set of answers.

The Mathematical Object

The weight matrices inside the neural network—the tables of numbers that the model learned during pretraining. Fine-tuning adjusts every entry in these tables, or sometimes just a small subset (using techniques like LoRA, which tweaks only a smaller 'adapter' layer to save memory). These numbers are what you inspect and change.

The Operation

Start with the pretrained model's weights. Feed it examples from your task (along with the correct or preferred output for each). Compare its prediction to your target. Compute the error (how far off it was). Use calculus to find which weight changes reduce that error. Make those small changes. Repeat until the model gets your task examples right.

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose a pretrained model sees 'Q: What is 2+2? A:' and predicts 'the number 4 was discovered by Pythagoras.' That is fluent but wrong—it learned what often follows '2+2' in raw text, not the math. Fine-tuning on arithmetic questions shows the model: this input should give 'A: 4'. By comparing its wrong guess ('Pythagoras') to the right answer ('4') on hundreds of such examples, the model's weights shift. Later, it sees 'Q: What is 5+3?' and now predicts 'A: 8' instead of a historical digression. The same math machinery learned math instead of trivia.

What the Lectures Emphasize

CME295 frames fine-tuning as the critical second stage after pretraining: the pretrained model has broad knowledge but isn't yet specialized for your task, so you use smaller amounts of high-quality task data to steer its behavior. CS224R then extends this to preference learning: fine-tuning can shape not just factual accuracy but tone, safety, and how the model balances competing goals, turning 'text prediction' into 'helpful assistant behavior'.

Common Misunderstanding

Many people think fine-tuning adds new facts or knowledge to a model. It mostly doesn't. Fine-tuning reshapes what the model already knows—making it favor task-relevant outputs, suppressing off-topic tangents, changing format or tone, or deciding between multiple correct answers. It can narrow the model if tuning data is limited. It can overwrite behaviors if not done carefully. But it is steering, not new learning.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In research papers, look for: a pretrained model being adapted with task-specific data, instruction examples, supervised demonstrations, or preference comparisons. Techniques like LoRA (a small adapter layer), prompt tuning (learning a prefix), or full weight adjustment all count—the shared idea is: start with a broad model, use feedback or examples for your task, adjust its internal weights, not redesign it.

Connected Concepts

Fine-tuning is the bridge between pretraining and deployment. In CME295, it is the natural second step for language models. In CME296 (diffusion/vision), similar techniques adapt pretrained vision models to new tasks. In CS224R, fine-tuning feeds into reward modeling and RL-for-LLMs: you learn a reward function (which outputs are 'good') and use it to further adjust the model, stacking feedback on feedback.
