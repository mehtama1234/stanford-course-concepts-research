Policy Gradient

A method for teaching a program to pick better actions by making the choices that led to good results happen more often, and making the choices that led to bad results happen less often.

What real-world problem is this about?

You're learning to cook by trial: no one tells you whether to add salt or sugar first, you just taste the final dish and decide if it's good. How do you figure out which specific moves (stir fast or slow, high heat or low, add this ingredient before that) actually made the difference between a meal you'd eat and one you'd throw away?

Why does this problem exist?

When you can try things and see what happens, the most direct path forward is to shift the odds: increase the chance your program picks actions that led to rewards, decrease the chance it picks actions that led to failure. You're not looking for one automatic action; you're learning to favor the ones that work.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Take how likely your program was to pick a particular action (convert it to a logarithm to make the math simpler), then multiply that by how much better the result turned out compared to what typically happens in that situation. If an action gave a reward of 100 and the program usually gets 70 in that spot, multiply the log-likelihood by +30; if it gave 40, multiply by -30. Do this for every action in every attempt, then push your program's dials in the direction that makes high-multiplier actions more likely and low-multiplier actions less likely.

Why is this concept important?

This sidesteps needing to know the exact value of every possible action beforehand. Your program can handle actions that vary smoothly (like any angle from -180 to +180 degrees on a steering wheel) or face zillions of choices, because it just learns which moves its current strategy should lean toward—not evaluate every single option.

What breaks without it?

You're stuck with problems where the number of actions is small and you can measure each one separately. Anything needing smooth, continuous control (a robot arm, a car steering), real-time decisions, or facing millions of options becomes impossible to solve by checking each action one by one.

Naive Starting Point

Someone might think: 'Just run the program, notice which actions got rewards, and boost those actions.' That skips a crucial step: does the action deserve credit, or was it just along for a lucky ride?

Why the Simple Approach Fails

If you reward every action from a successful attempt equally—even the mediocre ones that just happened to occur during a lucky streak—your program ends up favoring random noise alongside the real skill. It learns slowly and bounces around erratically, because it's reinforcing accidents as much as the choices that actually drove the win.

The Mathematical Object

A machine that reads the current situation and outputs how likely your program thinks each action is (on a log scale, which is just the logarithm of the probability). This is the object you adjust: push up the log-likelihood of actions that outperformed the average, push down the log-likelihood of actions that underperformed. The machine learns by nudging these outputs bit by bit.

The Operation

Run your program many times on real or simulated tasks. Each time it picks an action, calculate how much better the total future reward was compared to the program's baseline expectation at that moment. For every action, multiply its log-likelihood by this comparison (advantage). Adjust the program's dials to increase the log-likelihoods of high-advantage actions and decrease the log-likelihoods of low-advantage actions. Run again with the updated program.

Worked Mini-Example

A robot learning to walk toward a target. State: the target is 2 meters north. The robot's brain says: walking north has log-likelihood -0.92 (probability ~40%), walking east has log-likelihood -1.21 (probability ~30%), staying put has log-likelihood -1.21 (probability ~30%). The robot walks north and gets reward 10 (expected was 5 from experience, advantage +5). It walks east, gets reward 2 (expected 5, advantage -3). It stays put, gets reward 5 (expected 5, advantage 0). After this batch: north's log-likelihood increases by an amount proportional to +5, east decreases (advantage -3), staying stays the same (advantage 0). Over hundreds of episodes, the robot's brain increasingly favors north in this situation because north has the best track record.

What the Lectures Emphasize

CS224R treats policy gradient as the foundation of all learning-from-trial algorithms: gather experience by running your current program, compute the log-likelihood gradient weighted by how well each action performed relative to typical performance, adjust the program's numbers, and loop. Emphasis falls on a critical rule: the program must be the one generating the experience (not a past version, not random exploration)—otherwise the advantage calculation gets confounded. The course introduces reweighting tricks to fudge this rule for certain uses. The core: turning real-world outcomes into smarter behavior through the logarithmic gradient of probabilities.

Common Misunderstanding

Many assume policy gradient means 'if you get any reward, make that action more likely.' Wrong. The real rule is finer: only reinforce an action if it beat the program's average performance in that situation. An action that earns 50 points when the program typically earns 60 should actually become less likely, not more. Mixing up 'got a reward' with 'beat the baseline' breaks the whole method.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for this pattern whenever a paper multiplies action probabilities by reward signals or advantage scores (especially involving the log of probability). Methods like REINFORCE (the simplest version), PPO (a more stable version), actor-critic (using a helper network to estimate the baseline), and fine-tuning of text-generation AI all run this primitive underneath. The telltale: gradient flow running backward from final outcomes to make certain probability outputs grow and others shrink.

Connected Concepts

In diffusion models (the Diffusion course), you see sample-and-improve: create noisy pictures, score them against the target, nudge the model toward higher-scoring samples—same logarithmic feedback loop. In transformers and language models (the Transformers course), fine-tuning text generators follows this: generate text sequences, rate them, adjust the probability of each token up or down. Across the three courses, one pattern repeats: learn from actual sampled outputs rather than trying to evaluate every possibility in advance.
