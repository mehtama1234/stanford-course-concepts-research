Policy Gradient

A recipe for tuning your action strategy by making the choices that worked better more likely next time.

What real-world problem is this about?

You're learning to cook, but no one tells you which moves are 'right'—you only taste the result. How do you figure out which ingredients and techniques to favor, when you have to learn from trying things and seeing what happens?

Why does this problem exist?

If you can sample actions and see outcomes, the most direct path to improvement is to shift probabilities: boost the odds of actions that led to good outcomes, shrink the odds of actions from bad ones. You're not guessing the right action; you're learning which choices your policy favors.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

The gradient (direction of steepest climb) of the log probability of an action, weighted by how much better its outcome was than average. In symbols: push the probability of action a in state s upward by an amount proportional to (return - baseline), where 'log probability' means taking the log of how likely your policy thinks this action is, 'return' is the sum of future rewards, and 'baseline' is your policy's expected return, so you only reward actions that beat the benchmark.

Why is this concept important?

This breaks the bottleneck of needing to know the true value of every possible action. You can handle continuous actions (like steering angle at any value from -180 to +180 degrees) or huge action spaces by learning which choices your policy leans toward.

What breaks without it?

You'd be stuck in domains where action spaces are small and discrete, where you can afford to evaluate each action separately. Anything involving continuous motion, real-time control, or an ocean of possible choices stays intractable.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might think: 'Just run the policy, see which actions gave rewards, and flip a switch to make those actions more likely.' This skips the critical step of comparing against a baseline.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Without a baseline, you reinforce all actions from winning episodes equally—even mediocre moves mixed with lucky ones. The agent ends up favoring noise and high-variance outcomes instead of learning the actions that truly drive success, making learning slow and unstable.

The Mathematical Object

A table or neural-network layer that outputs the log probability (logarithm of likelihood) of each action given the current state. This is the object you nudge: increase the log probabilities of actions that beat the baseline, decrease the others.

The Operation

Collect a batch of episodes by running your current policy. For each action in each episode, compute how much better the return was than your policy's expected return at that state (the 'advantage'). Move the log probabilities of high-advantage actions upward and low-advantage actions downward. Repeat with fresh episodes.

Worked Mini-Example

A robot learning to reach a target. At state s (target is 2 meters north), the policy outputs log-probabilities: moving north log(0.4) = -0.92, moving east log(0.3) = -1.21, staying log(0.3) = -1.21. The agent tries north, gets reward 10 (beat expected 5, advantage +5). It tries east, gets reward 2 (missed expected 5, advantage -3). After this batch, the log-probability of north increases (advantage +5 pushes it up), and east decreases (advantage -3 pulls it down). Over episodes, the policy learns to favor north in this state.

What the Lectures Emphasize

CS224R grounds policy gradient as the first principled policy-improvement loop: run the policy to collect data, compute gradient of log probability weighted by advantage, step the policy parameters, and repeat. The lecture stresses that data must come from the current policy (on-policy constraint) and introduces importance weights to relax this for off-policy learning. The core insight is that policy gradient converts experience into a behavior change through the gradient of log probability.

Common Misunderstanding

Many people think policy gradient means 'make any rewarded action more likely.' The true mechanism is finer: an action is only reinforced if it outperformed the policy's average, not just if it earned some reward. A mediocre action in a lucky episode should not be strengthened.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Spot policy-gradient logic whenever a paper multiplies action probabilities by advantages, preferences, or reward signals (especially the log-probability gradient). Methods like REINFORCE, PPO, actor-critic, and LLM fine-tuning all use this primitive. The signature is: feedback flows backward from outcomes to shift action probabilities.

Connected Concepts

In Diffusion and Vision, sample-and-improve appears in diffusion model training: sample noise levels and denoisers, score them against the target, nudge the model toward high-scoring samples. In Transformers, the same pattern surfaces in LLM fine-tuning: sample tokens, grade them, adjust generation probabilities. Across all three, the core is learning from sampled decisions rather than exhaustive evaluation.
