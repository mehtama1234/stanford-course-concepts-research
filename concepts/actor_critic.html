Actor-Critic

A system with two separate learned parts: one that decides which action to take next, and one that keeps score of how good that action turned out compared to what you'd normally expect from that situation.

What real-world problem is this about?

You're learning to cook. After you make a dish and taste it, you need to know: did this recipe work because it's genuinely good, or did today's ingredients just happen to be fresh? You can't tell by comparing to your memory alone—some meals were good by accident, others were bad because you were tired. You need a steady measuring stick: a baseline that tells you what a 'normal good' tastes like from your current skill level.

Why does this problem exist?

When you try an action and see what happens, you face two separate jobs: picking which action to try, and grading whether that action was actually wise. If you solve both jobs with the same system, they tangle. The grading system gets pulled off balance by random luck. Splitting them keeps the grading system stable and unchanging while the action-picker gets clearer, more accurate feedback.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

The actor—the action-picker—is a learned program that looks at the current situation and outputs a choice of what to do, then shifts its choices toward picks that earned high grades. The critic—the grader—is a separate learned program that looks at the current situation and predicts what the total future reward will be from here, and adjusts its predictions to match what actually happens. The critical move: take the actual reward you got, subtract the critic's prediction of a 'normal reward' for that situation, and you get the advantage—the pure credit that belongs to your action, stripped of luck from the state itself. The actor learns by moving toward actions with positive advantage.

Why is this concept important?

Using this cleaner advantage signal instead of raw reward lets the actor learn much faster and stop bouncing around. Problems where rewards arrive late and scattered across many steps become solvable with real amounts of data. Real systems that control robots or fine-tune language models depend on this split because it cuts data hunger and learning time drastically.

What breaks without it?

Without splitting the grader from the picker, you're stuck updating the action-picker with raw, jumpy reward signals. This either demands enormous amounts of data—just to average out the noise—or causes the action-picker to swing wildly between overcorrection and stagnation, never settling on a useful strategy.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner thinks: 'If an action led to high reward, do it more; if it led to low reward, do it less.' This works on tiny toy problems where you get immediate, clean feedback. But it misses something deep: an action can look good only because you landed in a good situation, not because the action itself was clever. You need to see what a 'typical good outcome' is from your current spot to judge whether your action beat the odds.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

The naive method breaks because it cannot tell 'this action was smart' from 'I got lucky with my surroundings.' Imagine you walk down a street and find money on the ground. Did your navigation skill shine, or did you just happen to walk through a rich neighborhood? Without a comparison point—'money appears here this often'—you credit your choice when you should credit location.

The Mathematical Object

Two learned programs: the actor, which outputs probabilities for each action given the current situation, and the critic, which outputs a single number—the expected total future reward from the current situation. The critic's number is the concrete handle: it is what you subtract from real reward to strip away the baseline luck of the situation and leave only the action's true value.

The Operation

Step 1: Look at the current situation. Step 2: The actor generates an action choice. Step 3: Do that action and observe the reward and what the world looks like next. Step 4: The critic predicts what the future reward should be from where you just were. Step 5: Subtract the critic's prediction from the reward you actually got—that difference is the advantage. Step 6: If advantage is positive, push the actor slightly toward choosing that action again in this situation; if negative, push it away. Step 7: Also shift the critic's prediction toward the real reward you observed, so it gets better at guessing.

Worked Mini-Example

You're learning to brew coffee. Situation right now: you have whole beans, hot water, and a grinder. The actor chooses the action 'grind the beans for 3 seconds.' You brew and taste it: 7 out of 10 (pretty tasty). The critic guesses: 'from this starting point, the typical future reward is about 6.' Advantage: 7 minus 6 equals 1 (your action beat the baseline by 1 point). The actor's confidence in 'grind 3 seconds in this spot' goes up slightly. If the critic had predicted 7 instead, advantage would be 0, and the actor would not shift—right, because you hit the typical outcome, not exceeded it. Next time, the critic adjusts its guess from 6 toward 7 based on what you actually got.

What the Lectures Emphasize

The core progression here is: raw policy gradients are too wiggly, so you need a baseline to compare against; that baseline is a value function; separating the value function from the policy update gives you stability and opens up off-policy methods. The instructor emphasizes that the critic is not a luxury—it is the signal-cleaning machine that makes learning with real-world data tractable.

Common Misunderstanding

Many believe the critic should have access to a ground-truth answer and get it right from the start. Wrong. The critic is also learned and wrong at the beginning. It improves gradually. A broken critic—one that thinks every state is amazing, or thinks every state is terrible—will fool the actor into confidently chasing bad actions. Both systems improve together, and both stay imperfect.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for this pattern: one learned part picks actions or generates text, another learned part scores those outcomes or estimates their worth. You'll see it in language-model tuning (the language model picks, a reward model grades), robot control (one network outputs movement, another outputs state value), and off-policy methods. The fingerprint is this split between decision-making and evaluation.

Connected Concepts

Actor-critic grows out of earlier techniques: policy gradients (which taught how to tune an action-picker) and value functions (which taught how to estimate future reward). In language-model fine-tuning, the same actor-critic idea reappears—the language model is the actor, a reward model is the critic. In diffusion models and vision systems, the idea of learning a separate evaluator (a discriminator in older language) echoes the critic's job: it stands outside the main system and feeds back stable, independent judgment.
