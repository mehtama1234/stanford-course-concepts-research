Vision Transformers

A program that divides an image into small square sections, turns each section into a list of numbers, and then lets every section directly compare itself to every other section to figure out which ones matter for its understanding—doing all this comparison in a single pass instead of gradually spreading information through layer after layer.

What real-world problem is this about?

When you look at a photo to spot if a person is smiling, you're not just looking at the mouth. You glance at their eyes, the corners of their face, how their cheeks sit. All those details spread across the image. If your eye could only focus on tiny patches at a time without seeing how far-away parts connect, you'd miss the full picture of the smile.

Why does this problem exist?

Images work because shapes, colors, and textures in one place mean something only when linked to distant parts. A face's emotion lives in the relationship between eyes, nose, and mouth—scattered across space. A cat's paw only looks like a paw when connected to the leg it's attached to. Any image-understanding system that looks only at local neighborhoods will miss these connections and fail to recognize what's actually in the picture.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Slice the image into small rectangular pieces (each maybe 16×16 pixels). Write each piece as a list of numbers. For every piece, calculate how similar it is to every other piece by multiplying the numbers in one list by the numbers in another and summing the result (this measures how much they match). Shrink these scores by dividing by a fixed amount so the numbers stay reasonable. Run these scores through a function that turns them into percentages that add up to 100%. Now, for each piece, add up all the other pieces' number-lists, weighting each one by its percentage. Each piece's result is now a blend of information from the entire image, computed in one step.

Why is this concept important?

Instead of hand-coding which parts of an image should pay attention to which other parts, the model learns this on its own during training. The model discovers that for car images, wheels matter to the body, and for face images, eyes matter to the mouth. This single idea works for any image type, any task, and scales to models trained on billions of pictures.

What breaks without it?

Without letting distant parts talk directly, the model uses stacked filters that scan local neighborhoods. Information spreads slowly: the center of a large image can't 'know' about the edges until information travels inward through many layers, like a message passed hand-to-hand. Some patterns need layers and layers to connect. The model gets bottlenecked, and distant relationships stay hidden.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner's instinct is to treat images like computer vision traditionally did: apply the same small filter to every location, stack these layers, and assume enough layers will move information across the image. Local structure (edges, colors, textures) dominates locally, so this seems reasonable.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

In a 300-by-300 pixel image, a small filter applied 10 times only spreads information a short distance. The very center pixel needs roughly 100 layer passes to 'feel' the edges at the corners. For a billion-pixel satellite image, this becomes impossibly slow. Attention does the same reaching in one pass: every piece directly touches every other piece, no waiting for information to bounce between layers.

The Mathematical Object

A square table with one row and one column for each image piece. The cell in row i, column j holds a percentage representing how much piece j should contribute to piece i's final output. This table is computed fresh from the piece numbers and changes as the model learns during training, so the model tunes which regions should connect.

The Operation

Split the image into pieces and convert each to a number-list. For every piece, compute how similar it is to every other piece (dot product—multiply and sum). Take those scores, shrink them to keep numbers stable, turn them into percentages. For each piece, multiply all other pieces' number-lists by their percentages and add them up. The result is a new number-list for each piece that now carries information from the whole image.

Worked Mini-Example

Take a 4-by-4 image and split it into four 2-by-2 squares: top-left red, top-right blue, bottom-left green, bottom-right yellow. Convert each to a number-list: red is [0.8, 0.1], blue is [0.3, 0.9], green is [0.1, 0.8], yellow is [0.9, 0.1]. For the red square, compute how similar it is to each square: red matches red well (score 0.65), barely matches blue (0.05), lightly matches green (0.1), moderately matches yellow (0.2). Turn these into percentages: roughly 60% red, 10% blue, 10% green, 20% yellow. Red's new number-list is 0.6 times [0.8, 0.1] plus 0.1 times [0.3, 0.9] plus 0.1 times [0.1, 0.8] plus 0.2 times [0.9, 0.1], which gives [0.67, 0.25]—a mix that includes hints of blue, green, and yellow. In one step, the red square learned global context.

What the Lectures Emphasize

The CME296 lecture centers on how transformers, originally built for language, move into images by treating image-squares as tokens. A language model reads 'the cat sat on the mat' token by token. A vision transformer reads an image piece by piece, and each piece asks 'which other pieces matter for me?' using the same comparison-based method. This unified both languages and images under one architecture, which later enabled models that handle both text and pictures together. CME295 extends this further: the same mechanism that solves one input type (tokens, patches, anything divisible into pieces) solves others too.

Common Misunderstanding

Many assume vision transformers work because they simply copy what succeeded in language models. The truth is more specific: images need position encoding so the model remembers that top-left and bottom-right squares are far apart, not interchangeable. Patch size must be tuned (too small wastes computation, too large loses detail). Some models add extra visual structure that language models don't need. Splitting into pieces is the first step; the insight is much richer.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you see an image cut into pieces or 'visual tokens' that are then converted to number-lists and processed with attention blocks, vision transformers are working. Papers mentioning ViT (Vision Transformer), DiT (Diffusion Transformer used on images), or CLIP (which does attention over image pieces) use this. In medical scans or satellite photos, if the system divides the image and links all regions globally, vision transformers are the engine.

Connected Concepts

Vision transformers connect three threads: (1) Transformers from CME295—the attention mechanism that compares pieces; (2) Diffusion and images from CME296—diffusion models use transformer backbones to refine image features iteratively; (3) Reinforcement learning agents from CS224R—agents that process images often use attention-based encoders to understand their surroundings. Position encoding from the 'Position and Order' lesson tells the model where each piece sits in the image. Latent space from 'Latent Space' is the space where piece number-lists live. Attention is the backbone that ties it all together.
