Pretraining

A computer learns to recognize patterns by reading through billions of examples of text, code, or images—none labeled with answers—before it's ever asked to do a specific job. Think of it like reading your entire school's library before taking a single exam.

What real-world problem is this about?

You want to teach someone to write well. Paying experts to mark up essays is expensive and slow. But books, articles, and websites are everywhere. Someone who reads millions of words before writing learns rhythm, vocabulary, and style without anyone teaching it—they've absorbed it from examples.

Why does this problem exist?

Good answers to specific questions are hard to get and expensive to label. Raw examples—text, images, video—are free and everywhere. A system that learns from raw examples first will need far fewer expensive labeled examples later. This is not a choice; it's the only way to scale.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

The model has millions of internal dials called weights. It makes a guess (next word, or what a fuzzy image should look like), measures how wrong it was, and turns the dials a little in whatever direction makes it less wrong on that example. It repeats this billions of times on different examples. The measure of wrongness is called loss—a single number. Calculus tells you which direction each dial should turn to shrink that loss. When you practice predicting the next word a billion times, the model absorbs structure like grammar and facts without anyone teaching it explicitly.

Why is this concept important?

Pretraining builds a foundation of broad skill in a domain—language, images, or video. A model that has read millions of books learns how those domains work. When you later teach it a specific task with fewer labeled examples, it learns faster and needs less data. The knowledge transfers.

What breaks without it?

Every new task needs its own large set of hand-labeled answers. Models learn narrow, task-specific behavior from scratch. Knowledge does not transfer from one task to the next. You end up needing vastly more labeled examples and far more compute for each new application.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might write rules: if the previous word is a noun, expect a verb next. Or hire someone to hand-pick which features of an image matter. These work on tiny, clean datasets but collapse on real language or images—too many exceptions, too many unpredictable patterns for a human to list.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Rule-based systems break because language has exceptions to every rule. Hand-crafted features freeze what a model can learn—you have to guess which patterns matter before seeing the data, and you'll always be wrong about patterns you didn't anticipate. The model cannot discover new patterns or adjust when data changes.

The Mathematical Object

The model is a table of billions of numbers called weights or parameters. Each number contributes a tiny piece to the final answer. During training, the model rearranges these numbers—turning some up, some down—to shrink the loss. The numbers encode the patterns found in the data: how words relate to each other, how pixels form objects, how sound creates meaning.

The Operation

Start with a model whose weights are random. Feed it text or images from massive datasets one example at a time. For each example, compute the loss: how wrong was the prediction. Use calculus to find which direction each weight should move to make the loss smaller. Shift every weight a tiny amount in that direction. Repeat this billions of times across different examples until the loss stops getting smaller.

Worked Mini-Example

Imagine pretraining on Wikipedia. Show the model 'The cat sat on the ___' and it guesses 'cloud'. The loss is high because the answer is 'mat'. The model adjusts its weights to favor 'mat' in this context. After millions of sentences where 'noun sat on object', the weights encode that pattern. By the end, the model predicts the next word well—it's a reliable autocompleter. But it has no idea what 'helpful' means. That arrives later, in fine-tuning.

What the Lectures Emphasize

The lecture presents pretraining as the first, compute-heavy stage where a model reads billions of words and images to learn what language and images look like. The output is a model that predicts the next word or pixel accurately. The lecturer makes clear that pretraining alone does not create a helpful assistant—it creates knowledge without direction. That split is crucial: pretraining builds skill, but later stages add values.

Common Misunderstanding

People often think pretraining alone creates an AI assistant. The lecture corrects this: pretraining produces a model that knows language structure, but has no value system—no sense of helpful or harmful. Without further training toward specific behavior, the model completes any pattern it saw, whether wrong, biased, or dangerous. Pretraining is the first step, not the final product.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for pretraining whenever a paper describes a model learning from abundant raw data through a general task: predicting the next word, guessing hidden words in a sentence, denoising blurry images back to sharp ones, or learning which items are similar without being told. The thread is: practice on cheap, abundant feedback before specialization.

Connected Concepts

In CME295 (Transformers/LLMs), pretraining is the base before fine-tuning for specific tasks. In CME296 (Diffusion/Vision), diffusion models learn to clean up random noise in images before being steered toward a goal. Across all three: master the domain on raw signal first, then point the learned skill at a specific behavior.
