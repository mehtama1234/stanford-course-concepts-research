learning from feedback

RL For LLMs

A way to teach an AI language model to improve its answers by telling it which complete responses were good or bad, rather than showing it every correct word ahead of time.

What real-world problem is this about?

Imagine teaching a child to cook. You taste the final dish and say 'delicious' or 'this needs salt,' but you never watched their hands during cooking. The child must figure out which of their hundreds of choices—how long to stir, what heat to use, when to add salt—led to that bad taste. Feedback arrives only at the end, not step by step.

Why does this problem exist?

A language model writes text by choosing one word at a time, dozens or hundreds of words in a row. When you give a reward score only for the final answer, the model faces a puzzle: 'Which earlier words I chose made this ending good or bad?' This problem—figuring out which of your past choices led to a reward that arrives at the end—is the core challenge in this kind of learning.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Think of each word choice as a decision, and a whole response as a path of decisions. A reward score measures the final result (like: +1 if the math answer is right, 0 if wrong). The model's strategy is its probability over which word to pick next, given all the words so far. To improve, the model learns to boost the probability of words that showed up in high-reward responses. Technically, the model calculates a score for how wrong each word choice was (its log-probability), multiplies that score by the reward that eventually came from that word, and then nudges the internal numbers (weights) to make high-reward words slightly more likely next time. Why nudging? Because small updates in the direction of reward improve performance while keeping the model close to what it already learned.

Why is this concept important?

Feedback on whether a full answer is correct, or whether a human likes it, or whether a reasoning step makes sense—this feedback is cheap and quick to collect. But feedback on every single word choice is expensive and often impossible. This method unlocks training on tasks where a human can judge the final answer but cannot or should not have to write out every step.

What breaks without it?

Without connecting an outcome score back to the word choices that created it, a model can only copy patterns from examples of perfect text that humans already wrote. It cannot learn new things beyond those examples and cannot get better at new goals, like reasoning faster or writing more clearly or avoiding harmful outputs.

Naive Starting Point

Someone might think: 'If response A is good, just make the model more likely to output those exact same words in the same order.' This sounds straightforward—copy what worked.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

The trap is that many different paths through word choices can lead to the same correct answer, or the correct words depend on what came before. Copying response A's words without knowing which decision points in the middle were crucial teaches the model brittle, fragile behavior. Also, a reward score at word 1000 cannot directly change the word picked at word 10—you have to trace backward through the entire sequence to figure out which early words mattered.

The Mathematical Object

The model itself—specifically, the set of internal numbers (weights) that the model adjusts during training. These weights determine the probability distribution over possible next words given the words seen so far. Each training step tweaks these weights to make words that appeared in high-reward sequences slightly more probable.

The Operation

For each response the model generates: (1) Run the model to produce a complete response, word by word. (2) Score the response using a reward signal (does the math check out, does a human prefer it, is the reasoning sound). (3) Calculate a gradient—a direction to nudge the weights—for each word, scaled by the reward accumulated from that word onward. (4) Adjust the weights slightly in that direction. Repeat this thousands of times on different responses until the model's word probabilities shift toward high-reward patterns.

Worked Mini-Example

The model generates two responses: '5 plus 3 is 8' (correct, reward +1) and '5 plus 3 is 9' (wrong, reward 0). Which word choices deserve credit for the first response's success? All of them, but how much does the word '8' matter versus the word 'is' or '5'? The algorithm estimates by asking: how much reward remains after each word? The word '8' appears at the end and likely carries most of the credit for correctness, while '5' appeared early and contributed indirectly. Both get a boost in probability, weighted by their estimated contribution. Over thousands of examples, the model learns which word patterns consistently lead to correct answers and shifts its probabilities accordingly.

What the Lectures Emphasize

The course treats word generation as a series of decisions shaped by reward signals—each word is a choice, and the model learns to favor choices that led to good outcomes. The hardest piece is credit assignment: in a response of hundreds of words, which ones earned the final reward score? The course notes that simple 'right or wrong' rewards propagate poorly through long sequences, so modern work uses intermediate rewards that score partial progress, like checking if each reasoning step is sound, so the model can see which mid-reasoning words mattered.

Common Misunderstanding

Students assume this is just 'give the model a thumbs-up or thumbs-down.' The hard problem is that a thumbs-up at the very end must influence the probabilities of thousands of earlier word choices fairly and accurately. Without careful tracking of which words contributed to the final score, the model learns wrong shortcuts—like always writing longer responses because longer text happened to be correct in training data, not because length actually improves reasoning.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In a research paper, spot this method when you see phrases like 'reward model,' 'preference learning,' 'learning from feedback,' 'GRPO,' 'rejection sampling,' or 'verifier model.' The signal is always: the researchers must decide how to give credit for a score that came from the whole response back to each individual word choice within that response.

Connected Concepts

This concept connects to preference learning, which compares two responses to say which is better; this method turns that comparison into word-level training. It also connects to reasoning traces, where researchers structure intermediate feedback (scoring each step) so credit assignment becomes easier. In other domains, the same pattern appears: in image generation, training on picture quality means each pixel's update must be traced back to see how much it contributed to the final score.
