Credit Assignment

When you see an outcome, figuring out which choice from your past actually caused it so you can repeat the good choices and avoid the bad ones next time.

What real-world problem is this about?

You bake a cake that comes out burnt. The mistake wasn't adding the wrong ingredient five minutes ago—it was setting the oven temperature wrong an hour earlier. By the time you see the burnt cake, the real culprit is buried in history. How do you know which step to change next time to bake it right?

Why does this problem exist?

Everything that happens now is the result of a chain of choices stretching backward in time. If you blame or praise only the last thing you did, you miss the real causes. A system that learns needs a way to ask: which choice from steps or years ago actually created this outcome?

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

Imagine a machine learning from rewards that come later. It needs a trick: weight rewards that arrive soon more heavily than rewards that arrive far in the future. We use a discount factor—a number slightly less than 1, like 0.99—that shrinks the importance of distant rewards. The return (total sum of future rewards) is: R_t = r_t + γ·r_{t+1} + γ²·r_{t+2} + ..., where r is the immediate reward at each step, γ (gamma) is 0.99, and the power grows with each future step. This means the first future reward gets multiplied by 0.99, the next by 0.9801, and so on—recent rewards count more. The discount factor solves two problems: it mathematically shrinks distant rewards so learning focuses on nearby causes, and it matches intuition—a reward you get today matters more than one you'll get next year. Without discounting, adding up infinite future rewards gives infinity; with it, you get a real number the machine can actually use to learn.

Why is this concept important?

Most real tasks span long stretches of time. Writing code means hundreds of keystrokes before the program works. Playing chess means twenty moves before you win. Learning to cook means combining ingredients, heating, and timing across an hour. If a learner could only connect actions to rewards that happen seconds later, it would fail at everything. Without tracing consequences backward through time, the learner stays stuck, unable to improve plans that matter.

What breaks without it?

The system blames the last action for everything. A reward comes in, and the machine says: the thing I just did caused this. That's usually wrong. It overreacts to random noise right before the outcome and ignores the actual choice that shaped the result. Plans fall apart because there's no way to learn which earlier action was the turning point.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner might think: just check every past action and what reward it got right after. If an action gave a reward, make it more likely; if it gave a penalty, make it less likely. This works fine if rewards come instantly. But in real tasks, rewards are sparse—they arrive long after the choice that mattered.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

The naive method collapses instantly when reward is delayed. A robot picks up a block at step 1, waits 50 steps, then gets a reward signal at step 51. The naive learner has no connection between step 1 and step 51. It only sees the actions near step 51 and changes those. So it never learns that the block pickup at step 1 was crucial. The system gets trapped improving only the last few moves, missing the real pattern that created success.

The Mathematical Object

A table or function that stores the discounted return—a weighted sum of all future rewards—for each choice the learner encounters. As the learner tries actions and gets rewards, it fills in this table: for a situation S and action A at time t, the entry holds R_t = r_t + γ·r_{t+1} + γ²·r_{t+2} + ... This table is what the learner reads to decide which situations and actions are worth choosing. It's the concrete record that captures which past choices actually led to success.

The Operation

At each moment, the learner observes where it is and picks an action. It gets an immediate reward and moves to the next situation. Once a full sequence ends, the learner walks backward from the end: it starts with the final reward and works backward, multiplying each reward by the discount factor raised to the power of how many steps in the past it was. It updates its table using these computed values. Next time, actions with higher computed returns are chosen more often.

Worked Mini-Example

A robot learns to stack three blocks. Step 0: pick up red block (reward = 0). Step 1: position blue block (reward = 0). Step 2: place red on blue (reward = 0). Step 3: place yellow on red (reward = +100, goal complete). Using discount factor γ = 0.9, compute returns backward: R₃ = 100, R₂ = 0 + 0.9 · 100 = 90, R₁ = 0 + 0.9 · 90 = 81, R₀ = 0 + 0.9 · 81 = 72.9. Now update: step 3's action (place yellow) gets reinforced with weight 100 (direct success). Step 2's action (place red on blue) gets reinforced with weight 90—slightly less because it's one step removed but still crucial. Step 1's action (position blue) gets reinforced with weight 81; step 0's action (pick red) gets reinforced with weight 72.9. All four steps contributed to the final success, so all four become more likely next time. If you only reinforced step 3, the robot would randomly stumble onto the right stack maybe once in a thousand episodes. By assigning credit to all steps in the chain, learning accelerates 100×. The discount factor ensures earlier steps are weighted less (they are further from the outcome), but not zero.

What the Lectures Emphasize

CS224R teaches that reinforcement learning is hard precisely because rewards arrive late or almost never. The course covers problems where success comes many steps or even many months after the key decision. When explaining returns and discount factors, the lecture drives home that these numbers—less than 1—are the mathematical tool for tracing backward from outcomes to earlier choices, giving heavier weight to recent consequences so learning stays connected to reality.

Common Misunderstanding

People often assume: the action right before the reward gets the most credit. That's almost always wrong. The action right before reward might be trivial; the real turning point could be twenty steps earlier. Discounting spreads responsibility backward through time, not to the last action—it asks which entire sequence of past choices led here.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

When you read a research paper or code, look for this idea whenever outcomes are delayed or sequences are long. Watch for words like return, discount factor, temporal-difference error, advantage function, process reward, or trajectory—these signal the machinery at work. Even if the paper doesn't name it, if it's figuring out which past actions mattered for the outcome, that's this idea.

Connected Concepts

In Transformers and language models, attention solves a similar question: which earlier text chunks matter for understanding this word right now? In diffusion models that generate images, the denoising task asks: which earlier steps of noise removal shaped what I see now? In Deep Reinforcement Learning, this idea asks: which earlier actions caused this outcome? The deep pattern is the same across all three: figure out what happened in the past to make sense of what's happening now.
