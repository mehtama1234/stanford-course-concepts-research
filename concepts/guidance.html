Guidance

A technique to steer a generative model toward a specific outcome—like a text prompt, label, or quality measure—while keeping outputs realistic and diverse.

What real-world problem is this about?

You ask a friend to draw a teddy bear reading a book. They return with a beautiful bear, but it's alone—no book. The drawing is skillful and realistic, but it missed your actual request. You need a way to keep their artistry while forcing attention to the book.

Why does this problem exist?

A generative model learns to make plausible things: if it sees a million bear images, it will draw bears well. But plausible is not the same as targeted. When you add a condition—a text prompt, a label, a safety rule—the model must balance: stay realistic (use what it learned about bears) while also satisfying the constraint (include a book). No single fixed rule can handle all cases. You need a mechanism that bends the generation process toward the condition at every step.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

During generation, the model builds an output step-by-step by predicting which direction reduces noise. Guidance works by modifying that direction using a score—a number that measures how well the current partial output satisfies your condition. If you want a bear reading a book, at each step you compute: "How much does this draft match the book-reading concept?" Then you blend the model's natural prediction with a scaled boost in the direction of higher match. Mathematically, the adjusted prediction is the original direction plus a weight times the gradient (slope) of your condition score. The weight controls the strength: too weak and you ignore your condition; too strong and outputs look unnatural because you overstrain the model away from what it learned.

Why is this concept important?

Without guidance, text-to-image and text-to-video systems would generate things that happen to be realistic but ignore your input entirely. Guidance is what makes these models practical—what lets you request a teddy bear reading a book and actually get both elements. It unlocks conditional generation as a user-facing tool rather than a research curiosity.

What breaks without it?

The model produces outputs that are visually coherent but irrelevant to your request. A beautiful bear with no book. A sunset-colored painting when you asked for a blue one. The output is realistic—nothing is physically impossible—but useless because it does not address your goal.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner thinks: why not just train a separate model to directly map text prompts to images? Or store examples in a lookup table? These approaches work on toy problems with tiny, clean inputs. But real requests are open-ended, and the space of possible conditions is infinite—you cannot precompute or memorize every combination.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

Direct mapping and lookup tables require either retraining for every new prompt (expensive) or a finite catalog of pre-computed results (brittle, boring). Once users ask for variations—different styles, unexpected combinations, nuanced adjectives—the system runs out of examples. A one-size-fits-all rule (like "just make it match the label") overshoots and creates images that are unnatural, distorted, or visually incoherent because the model is forced too hard against its learned patterns.

The Mathematical Object

A condition score—a neural network or function that takes a partial output (an image in the middle of generation) and produces a number measuring how well it satisfies your constraint. For text conditions, this might be a text-image similarity score; for safety, it might be a classifier that detects unsafe content. This score is differentiable (you can compute how it changes with small changes to the image), so you can measure the slope—the gradient—showing which direction improves adherence.

The Operation

At each generation step: (1) sample a candidate direction where the model naturally wants to go; (2) compute your condition score for the partial output; (3) compute the gradient of that score—the direction of increasing match; (4) blend the two directions using a weight parameter, producing an adjusted prediction that incorporates both the model's learned realism and your condition; (5) take one small step in that direction; (6) repeat until done.

Worked Mini-Example

You want an image of a green apple. A diffusion model starts from pure noise and removes noise step by step. At step 50, the model has a blurry reddish circular object—it thinks apple, but the color is wrong. Your condition score (comparing the image to the phrase "green apple") gives a gradient showing "shift toward green." Guidance scales this gradient and mixes it with the model's natural noise-removal step. The next step combines both: the model's instinct ("keep it apple-shaped") and your condition ("make it greener"). A few steps later, the apple is both plausibly apple-shaped and visibly green. Without guidance, the model might output a beautiful red apple, ignoring the "green" part.

What the Lectures Emphasize

CME296 emphasizes that visual quality and prompt adherence are separate: an image can be aesthetically excellent but fail the prompt. The evaluation lecture pairs this insight with classifier-free guidance, a practical method that emphasizes the text prompt during generation without needing a separate classifier model. The message is that controllable generation requires steering, not just training better models.

Common Misunderstanding

Many assume that a better generative model automatically follows prompts better. But a model trained only on diverse images becomes good at making plausible things, not at respecting your specific request. The model has learned thousands of visual patterns (bears with food, bears in forests) but no direct link to your words. Guidance is the missing bridge: it wires your condition directly into the generation trajectory.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

Look for methods that modify the generation path using scores or gradients of a condition. Classifier-free guidance uses the difference between conditioned and unconditioned predictions; reward-guided generation uses a reward score; safety guidance uses a classifier for harmful content; layout-guided generation uses spatial scores; and adversarial guidance uses discriminator feedback. All share the same skeleton: measure how well the current draft meets your goal, compute the direction of improvement, and blend it into generation.

Connected Concepts

In CS224R (Deep RL), guidance resembles reward shaping—modifying rewards to steer the agent toward desired behavior. In Transformers/LLMs, prompt engineering does something similar: it steers token predictions toward a goal by carefully choosing input text. Across all three courses, the shared thread is feedback: take a direction the model naturally follows, measure how well it meets your goal, and adjust accordingly.
