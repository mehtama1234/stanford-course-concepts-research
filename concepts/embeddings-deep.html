Embeddings — every word becomes a point in space where meaning is distance

A computer deals in numbers. Words are symbols. How does a computer turn the word 'king' into numbers that somehow capture that it means something like 'queen' and nothing like 'bicycle'? Embeddings are the answer: each word gets a list of numbers — a coordinate in a high-dimensional space — and training places related words near each other. Nobody writes the rule; the model learns it. Here is how the transform works, what it looks like on the real GPT-2, why geometry captures meaning, what breaks, and where this same idea runs through every modern AI system.

1 · The problem — words are not numbers, but computers speak only numbers

A computer's brain is arithmetic. It adds, multiplies, compares numbers. But language is made of symbols — words — which have meaning, feeling, and relationship. The word 'queen' is related to 'king' in a way that 'bicycle' is not. How do you encode that into arithmetic so a machine can work with it?

You could try assigning each word a single number: 'king' = 5, 'queen' = 6, 'bicycle' = 200. But a single number cannot hold enough information. It cannot say 'king and queen are related but different' while also saying 'both are different from bicycle'. A single number is a line, one dimension. Meaning is more complex than that. You need more dimensions.

2 · The reframe — give each word a coordinate in a learned space

The move: instead of one number per word, use a list — a vector, a point in high-dimensional space. For instance, 'king' might be the point [0.2, 0.8, −0.1, 0.5, ...], and 'queen' might be [0.19, 0.81, −0.08, 0.52, ...]. These two points are close together (small distance), while 'bicycle' sits far away at [−0.6, 0.2, 0.9, −0.3, ...]. The model learns these coordinates during training, by adjusting them so that words that tend to appear near each other in real language end up near each other in this space.

To measure how close two points are, you compute the cosine: a number between 0 (completely different direction) and 1 (identical direction). If 'king' and 'queen' have a cosine of 0.66, they point nearly the same way. If 'king' and 'bicycle' have a cosine of 0.22, they point in almost opposite ways. The space itself is learned — nobody hand-codes what direction 'royalty' should be or 'vehicle'. The model discovers it from the data.

3 · Seen on a real run — related words sit close, unrelated ones far apart

We took the actual GPT-2, a real pretrained language model with 124 million learned numbers, and looked at its embeddings. Each of its 50,257 tokens — every possible word or sub-word piece in its vocabulary — is a point in 768-dimensional space (768 numbers per word). We measured the cosine similarity between pairs of words to see if related words really do sit near each other.

king / queen0.66
king / man0.37

The discovered fact: related words cluster tightly — Monday and Tuesday sit at 0.89, king and queen at 0.66 — while unrelated pairs spread far apart: king and bicycle at only 0.22. The model never saw an explicit rule 'make king and queen close'. It learned this structure entirely from predicting the next word in billions of sentences. Meaning became geometry.

4 · Why geometry captures meaning

Why does this work? Because meaning lives in relationships, and relationships translate into space. In the real world, 'king' and 'queen' share structure: they are both royalty, both singular rulers, both sit in the social hierarchy at the same level. The model, while learning to predict text, discovers that these commonalities matter. So it places them close. When you later use these embeddings to understand a new sentence, the closeness means the model's learning carries across — it can apply what it learned about 'king' to problems involving 'queen' because the vectors are already similar.

This is called transfer: a vector space is a map of how words relate. The model does not hand-code this map; mathematics does the rest. Distance in this space corresponds to how the model predicts words should interact. It is learned, so it is exact for this model's view of language, not some idealized external standard.

5 · The one real weakness — one vector cannot hold multiple meanings

Here is where it breaks: each word gets only one vector. But words have multiple meanings. 'Bank' in 'river bank' is not the same as 'bank' in 'savings bank'. In GPT-2, both use the exact same embedding at the input layer. A single point cannot be in two places at once.

The model handles this partly by remaking each word's representation as it reads. After seeing the context 'river', the word 'bank' gets transformed — its vector gets adjusted by the layers of attention and processing that follow. So context does reshape meaning as the model reads forward. But at the input, a polysemous word (one with many meanings) starts as a single blur of all its senses. This is why context-dependent representation — how meaning shifts through the layers — matters so much in deep transformers.

6 · The surprise — embeddings are the foundation for search, recommendation, and learning itself

This single idea — 'turn symbols into vectors, let meaning become distance' — is far bigger than language. Almost every modern AI system uses embeddings or the same core idea. When you search for a phrase and the search engine returns documents about the same topic but not your exact words, it is computing embeddings of your query and the documents, then ranking by distance. When a music app recommends a song you have not heard, it is finding songs with similar embeddings in a space learned from what millions of people played. When a retrieval system finds relevant past examples to help another AI model, it is embedding both the new problem and the past examples, then picking the closest ones. When you build a attention layer (which blends a word by looking back), you are comparing the embeddings of the current word to the embeddings of all earlier words to see which ones matter.

Even more: embeddings are not just an output; they are the raw material of learning itself. As a model trains, these vectors change. A latent space is the learned representation a model builds by embedding input into a high-dimensional space. The idea applies to images (vision transformers embed patches of pixels as vectors), to code, to any symbolic system. Start here — meaning as geometry, distance as relationship — and you are holding the key that opens almost every modern learned system.

7 · Where it is used in practice — turning words and items into coordinates in learned space where meaning is distance

Search engines embed your query and all documents in the same space, then return the closest documents. Music streaming services embed songs and users in a space where similar listening patterns place them nearby, enabling recommendations. Chatbots use embeddings to retrieve relevant past conversations or documents to add context to a new question. Code search tools embed functions and queries to find the most similar code snippets.

Each word, song, user, or document becomes a coordinate in a learned space (a vector). Distance in this space (measured by cosine similarity or other metrics) tells you how related two items are. The model learns these coordinates by adjusting them so that items that co-occur or are semantically related end up nearby. A user who played similar songs has a vector close to other users with matching taste; a document about dogs and the word 'puppy' sit near each other.

Meaning becomes a learnable geometry, so any two items can be compared by position, and the model automatically discovers which relationships matter without being told a rule.

the run recipe — reproduce every number above

Run the real pretrained GPT-2 (124M) and extract its embedding matrix (50,257 tokens × 768 dimensions). For five word pairs (king/queen, king/man, dog/cat, king/bicycle, Monday/Tuesday), compute the cosine similarity between their embedding vectors. The cosine is the dot product of the two vectors divided by their lengths, yielding a number from 0 to 1.

embedding(word) = vector in R^768, learned during training cosine(u, v) = (u · v) / (||u|| × ||v||) # dot product, normalized by lengths measured cosines: king/queen = 0.66 king/man = 0.37 dog/cat = 0.55 king/bicycle = 0.22 Monday/Tuesday = 0.89
