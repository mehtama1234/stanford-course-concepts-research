Embeddings

A table of learned numbers that assigns every token, patch, or state a unique location in a multidimensional space, where distance and direction carry meaning.

What real-world problem is this about?

You walk into a library and find two books labeled only by number: 4129 and 983. The numbers tell you nothing about whether they are novels or cookbooks, whether one is related to the other, or what to read next. You need a system that captures what each book is actually about.

Why does this problem exist?

When a computer system processes discrete items (words, images, actions), it starts with arbitrary labels. To learn useful relationships—which words appear together, which states lead to reward, which images show similar objects—the system must place these items in a space where similarity becomes measurable by geometry. Otherwise, the system cannot smoothly adjust what it knows when feedback arrives.

What is the mathematical idea underneath?

An embedding is a learned function that maps each discrete item (like a word) to a vector, a list of numbers. If you have an embedding table E with dimensions (vocabulary_size, embedding_dimension), and you want the vector for word w, you look up row w in that table: v_w = E[w]. Training adjusts all the numbers in E so that vectors for related items end up close to each other. Closeness is measured by dot product: v_w · v_x = sum of (element_i in v_w times element_i in v_x). Large dot product means the vectors point in similar directions, capturing semantic or functional similarity learned from data.

Why is this concept important?

Once words (or patches, or states, or actions) live in a learned vector space, the model can compute similarity by dot product, retrieve nearest neighbors, average vectors to generalize, and measure what changed during learning. Attention mechanisms use embeddings to compare context; classification heads project embeddings to predictions; retrieval systems find related chunks by vector distance. Embeddings are the bridge from discrete symbols to continuous mathematics.

What breaks without it?

Without embeddings, the model sees only raw discrete IDs: 4129, 983, 2754. It has no way to express that two words are related or that feedback should transfer from one to another. Each item is isolated. The model cannot share learned patterns across similar inputs or build representations that enable downstream tasks like translation, question-answering, or control.

Naive Starting Point

A beginner asks: why not just use the integer ID directly, or assign each word a one-hot vector (a vector with one 1 and all 0s for each different word)? That avoids training anything and seems simpler.

Why the Simple Approach Fails

One-hot vectors do not capture similarity: the one-hot for "cat" has nothing in common with the one-hot for "kitten." Using raw IDs is even worse—the number 4129 for "cat" and 983 for "dog" are just arbitrary labels with no relationship. As the model learns from data, it has no mechanism to make related words push their representations together. Patterns cannot transfer from "cat" to "kitten." A truly learned, continuous representation is required.

The Mathematical Object

The embedding table is an actual matrix you can inspect and change. If your vocabulary has 50,000 words and you choose embedding_dimension = 768, the embedding table E is a 50,000 by 768 matrix of real numbers. During training, gradient descent updates every number in this matrix. You can visualize it: reduce E to two dimensions and plot words as points; related words cluster together. You can extract one row (a single word's embedding) and show it or use it for downstream computation.

The Operation

To use embeddings during inference: (1) tokenize the input text into word IDs like [4129, 2754, 983]. (2) Look up each ID in the embedding table to get its vector. (3) Pass these vectors to the next stage (attention mechanism, classifier, or retrieval module). During training, feedback from the model's final predictions flows backward, adjusting the numbers in the embedding table so that related words end up close.

Worked Mini-Example

Suppose you have a tiny vocabulary: "cat" (ID 4129), "dog" (ID 983), and "meow" (ID 7634). Initially, the embedding table assigns random vectors to each. During training, the model sees "cat meow" and "dog bark" in its training data. The attention mechanism learns that 'cat' and 'meow' often appear together, so it rewards their vectors being similar; similarly for 'dog' and 'bark.' After training, when you extract the three vectors and compute their dot products, v_cat · v_meow > 0 (high similarity, positive reward), while v_cat · v_dog might be moderate (both animals, some shared features). If a human reduces the 768-dimensional embeddings to 2D for visualization, they see 'cat' and 'dog' clustered together, separate from 'meow' and 'bark' (actions), revealing what the model learned.

What the Lectures Emphasize

In CME295 (Transformers), the lecture stresses that embeddings are the first numerical representation after tokenization: once text splits into tokens, those tokens must be converted to coordinates before any transformer operation. The example of BERT shows embeddings are expressive enough to power downstream tasks like classification. In retrieval-augmented generation, embeddings are explicitly the mechanism for lookup—when searching for relevant context, the system embeds the query as a vector and retrieves chunks whose embeddings are close. Across courses, embeddings reveal a unifying pattern: any item (token, patch, state) must first become a position in learned space for the algorithm to operate.

Common Misunderstanding

Many assume an embedding somehow encodes a human-readable definition—that if you could read the 768 numbers in a word's embedding, they would mean something like 'is_animate=0.8, is_noun=0.9.' In reality, embeddings are opaque bundles of learned weights. Two vectors being close means the training process pushed them together because they were useful in related computational contexts, not because they share one obvious property. Individual dimensions rarely have interpretable meaning.

How to Recognize This in a New Paper or Model

In a research paper, spot embeddings whenever raw discrete items (tokens, image patches, states, actions, documents, or even preferences) are first mapped into a continuous learned vector space before the main algorithm runs. The label may be 'encoding,' 'representation,' 'latent codes,' or 'basis expansion.' The signature is always the same: discrete input → look up in learned table → continuous vectors → downstream computation. Watch for phrases like 'we embed queries and documents' or 'state representation is learned end-to-end.'

Connected Concepts

Embeddings are the concrete realization of representation learning across all three courses. In CME295 (language), embeddings follow tokenization and feed attention. In CME296 (diffusion), the move from discrete object classes to positions in continuous latent space mirrors embeddings. In CS224R (RL), state and action embeddings let value networks and policies generalize across similar situations. The shared thread: convert identity into geometry so that learning can move things toward useful locations.
